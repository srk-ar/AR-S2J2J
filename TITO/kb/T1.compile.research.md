### SLOT 1: Relevant Standards and Guidance Documents

For a South African mining or tailings storage facility report, the standards hierarchy should be stated conservatively: local legal approvals, permit conditions, mine blasting procedures, and any project-specified South African National Standard control compliance wording, while external standards can support measurement practice, interpretation, and benchmarking. Where no project record supplies the local standard text or a project-specific acceptance criterion, the report should avoid presenting an externally sourced number as a South African compliance criterion. The strongest directly cited international baseline for fixed-structure vibration is ISO 4866:2010, which establishes principles for vibration measurement and data processing for evaluation of effects on structures, applies to structures above and below ground including earth structures such as dykes and embankments, and addresses sources including explosions and construction activities, while excluding some special structures including dams. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref003]

The most relevant international documents for report wording are ISO 4866:2010 for structural-vibration measurement and evaluation principles, BS 7385-2:1993 for building damage assessment from groundborne vibration, BS 6472-2:2008 for human exposure to blast-induced vibration in buildings, ISO 2631-2:2026 for human comfort and annoyance from whole-body vibration in buildings, the U.S. Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement rules in 30 CFR 816.67 and 30 CFR 816.68 as a clear regulatory example of PPV, scaled-distance, monitoring, and blast-record requirements, the IFC Environmental, Health, and Safety Guidelines for Mining as good international industry practice for mining noise, vibration, blasting, and tailings management, and the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management for TSF knowledge-base, design, governance, and monitoring context. [@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref007][@Ref008][@Ref001][@Ref002]

### SLOT 2: Criteria and Reporting Distinctions

The cited sources separate structural damage criteria from human response criteria. BS 7385-2 is a building damage document: it gives guidance on assessing the possibility of vibration-induced damage in buildings, covers measurement, recording, analysis, and damage records, and is limited to ground-transmitted vibration rather than vibration from machinery inside a building. BS 6472-2 is a human-exposure document for blast-induced vibration in occupied buildings, especially periodic blasting associated with mineral extraction, and it expressly does not address equipment malfunction, building damage, occupant injury, legal liability, or vibration minimization. ISO 2631-2:2026 similarly concerns comfort and annoyance from whole-body vibration in buildings, states that structural damage is discussed in ISO 4866 rather than in ISO 2631-2, and states that acceptable magnitudes are not set in that document. [@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref006]

Geotechnical effects and TSF performance should be reported as a separate engineering question rather than folded into building-damage or human-annoyance criteria. The IFC mining guidance treats waste dumps, tailings impoundments, and dams as facilities whose geotechnical risks and environmental impacts require risk assessment, recognized tailings standards, independent review, and ongoing physical and water-quality monitoring, while the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management requires an interdisciplinary knowledge base including geotechnical and seismicity information, documented design criteria, and integrated performance monitoring. By contrast, 30 CFR 816.67 gives protected-structure PPV and scaled-distance provisions for specified occupied structures and separately requires other structures such as dams, impoundments, utilities, tunnels, and underground mines to be protected through maximum allowable vibration limits submitted in the blasting plan and approved by the regulator. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref007]

Monitoring and reporting requirements are another distinct category. ISO 4866 supports measurement and processing principles for structural-vibration evaluation, BS 7385-2 supports measurement, recording, analysis, and damage-record procedures for buildings, and 30 CFR 816.68 provides a concrete blast-record template that includes location, date and time, blaster details, distance to protected structures, blast pattern sketches, burden, spacing, decks, delay pattern, hole dimensions, explosive type, explosive weight per hole, maximum weight detonated in an 8-millisecond period, initiation system, stemming, protective measures, and seismographic and airblast record details where required. [@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref008]

### SLOT 3: Charge Per Delay and Charge Window Definitions

Maximum charge per delay and maximum instantaneous charge should not be treated as self-defining terms in professional report text. In the clearest cited regulatory formulation, 30 CFR 816.67 defines the charge term used in its scaled-distance equation as the maximum weight of explosives detonated in any 8-millisecond period, and 30 CFR 816.68 requires the blast record to include the maximum weight of explosives detonated in an 8-millisecond period. This anchors the reported charge to a defined short-delay time window rather than to the total blast mass, the total explosive mass in a blast design, or a nominal delay number that may contain more than one hole or deck. [@Ref007][@Ref008]

For report purposes, charge per delay should therefore be defined explicitly as the summed explosive mass assigned to the relevant delay or to the relevant rolling charge window, with the chosen convention stated in the methods. Maximum instantaneous charge can be used as a synonym only if the report defines it as the maximum explosive mass assumed to detonate together under that same window rule. A short-delay charge window should also state whether the calculation uses nominal design delays, measured firing times, or vendor timing tolerances; whether the window is rolling or grouped by labelled delay; and whether endpoints are included, because those choices can change the maximum reported charge without changing the physical blast. [@Ref007][@Ref008]

### SLOT 4: Status of a 9 ms Charge Window

The cited standards and regulatory sources support an 8-millisecond charge period, not an independent 9 ms charge-window standard. The 8-millisecond period appears in both the 30 CFR 816.67 scaled-distance provision and the 30 CFR 816.68 blast-record provision, whereas the cited ISO, BSI, IFC, and GISTM sources do not establish a 9 ms charge-window definition for blast-vibration reporting. On that evidence, a 9 ms charge window should be treated as project-specific, software-specific, or vendor-specific unless the project blasting procedure, contract, permit, or monitoring method statement expressly defines it. [@Ref007][@Ref008][@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref001][@Ref002]

A 9 ms separation rule is not the same thing as a 9 ms charge window. If a method applies an 8-millisecond rolling window to integer-millisecond nominal delays, charges separated by at least 9 ms may fall outside a single 8-millisecond grouping under that implementation; however, that is a boundary convention derived from the 8-millisecond rule, not evidence that all charges in a 9 ms period must be summed. Report text should therefore say "maximum charge within any 8 ms period" when that is the adopted basis, or should define the alternative 9 ms calculation as a project assumption without calling it a standard criterion. [@Ref007][@Ref008]

### SLOT 5: Dual-Blast or Multi-Design Events

Dual-blast or multi-design events should be described by pairing charge and distance within each blast design before any comparison, scaled-distance calculation, or source attribution is made. The eCFR scaled-distance provision defines the charge variable and distance variable in the same equation, and the blast-record provision requires the record to include the blast location, distance to protected structures, pattern sketches, delay pattern, explosive weights, initiation system, and seismographic records where required. That structure supports report tables in which each design or blast source has its own distance to the monitor or protected structure, its own maximum charge in the stated delay window, and its own timing relationship to the measured vibration record. [@Ref007][@Ref008]

Where two blast designs contribute to one monitoring event, the measured PPV or other vibration output can be reported as an event-level measurement, but source-specific interpretation should be made only where timing, trigger records, waveform arrivals, or blast logs support attribution. It is not methodologically sound to combine the largest charge from one design with the shortest distance from another design as though they formed a physical source-receptor pair, unless the report labels the result as a deliberately conservative envelope rather than as the scaled distance of an actual blast design. If delay schedules overlap at the receptor, the charge-window calculation should be checked for each individual design and, where timing information supports it, for the combined event across the same stated rolling window. [@Ref007][@Ref008]
