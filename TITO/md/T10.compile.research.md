# Amplitude Scaling of Measured Blast Vibration Records for Tailings Storage Facility Dynamic Response Review

Project AR-S2J2J.R -- Technical Research Note T10

---

## SLOT 1: Support for Amplitude-Scaling Measured Records

The use of real recorded ground motion time series, adjusted to a target amplitude level by multiplication with a scalar factor, is a well-established procedure in geotechnical and structural dynamic analysis. Bommer and Acevedo (2004) provide a comprehensive treatment of the use of real accelerograms as input to dynamic analyses, documenting amplitude scaling as a permissible and widely applied adjustment method that retains the duration, frequency content, phase structure, and non-stationary character of the original measurement while proportionally modifying its intensity level. [@Ref001] The NIST GCR 11-917-15 report on selecting and scaling earthquake ground motions for nonlinear dynamic analysis formalizes amplitude scaling as a standard component of the ground motion preparation workflow, defining it explicitly as multiplication of the recorded acceleration time series by a constant scalar, and establishes guidance on the selection of records and the application of scale factors in a way that preserves the physical character of the original waveform. [@Ref002]

For blast vibration records, the rationale for amplitude scaling is grounded in the same physical principle that underlies earthquake record scaling: a measured blast vibration record contains the combined signature of the explosive source, the transmission path through rock and soil, and the site response at the measurement location. When the engineering objective is to define a scenario demand history at a specified amplitude level, multiplying the measured record by a scalar preserves all of these waveform characteristics while adjusting only the overall intensity. This approach is consistent with the measurement and reporting framework described in blast vibration standards, which characterize measured records in terms of peak particle velocity, waveform attributes, and distance-and-charge-dependent attenuation, treating the recorded waveform as the physical output of a specific source-path-site combination. [@Ref003] The transient, non-stationary character of blast acceleration histories -- with a rapid rise, a dominant pulse region, and a decaying tail -- is preserved through amplitude scaling in a manner that is not replicable by idealized harmonic or sinusoidal waveforms of equivalent amplitude.

In dam and embankment geotechnical analysis, amplitude scaling of measured seed records is accepted as one of the two principal methods for preparing ground motion inputs, the other being spectral matching. The distinction between them, and the specific advantages of amplitude scaling for preserving natural record variability, are described in the dam-specific guidance literature as follows: linear scaling applies a constant time-domain factor so that all waveform characteristics except amplitude are unchanged, whereas spectral matching modifies the frequency content and energy distribution of the record. [@Ref004] For a tailings storage facility dynamic response review in which the objective is to construct scenario demand histories at defined amplitude levels from existing measured blast records, amplitude scaling is the method that most directly achieves that objective: it adjusts the scenario amplitude without altering the waveform structure of the measured event, and it requires no spectral target that does not exist for near-field blast loading. The blasting and geotechnical transient dynamics literature supports the broader principle that measured records scaled to scenario amplitudes constitute a defensible basis for dynamic response assessment when the source-path-site conditions of the measured event are representative of the scenario loading environment. [@Ref002][@Ref001]

## SLOT 2: Preserved and Non-Preserved Properties

Amplitude scaling is defined by the operation $a_s(t) = SF \cdot a(t)$, where $a(t)$ is the measured acceleration time series and $SF$ is a constant scalar scale factor. Because the scale factor is constant in time and does not depend on frequency, the operation is linear and time-invariant. The Fourier transform of the scaled record $A_s(f) = SF \cdot A(f)$ preserves both the shape of the Fourier amplitude spectrum and the Fourier phase spectrum; only the absolute amplitude at each frequency changes, by the same factor $SF$. As a direct consequence, the normalized spectral shape -- the ratio of amplitude at any frequency to the amplitude at any reference frequency -- is unchanged. [@Ref005] Duration metrics that depend on relative amplitude thresholds are also preserved: the bracketed duration, defined by the interval between threshold crossings at a fixed fraction of peak amplitude, is unchanged because the fraction is maintained after scaling. The significant duration $D_{5-95}$, defined as the interval over which the Arias intensity accumulates from five to ninety-five percent of its total, is equally preserved, because the Arias intensity scales uniformly by $SF^2$ (each squared amplitude sample in the accumulation scales by the same factor), and the percentile times that define $D_{5-95}$ are determined by the shape of the normalized cumulative energy curve rather than its absolute level; that shape is identical in the original and scaled records. [@Ref002]

Phase relationships within the record are fully preserved. The temporal ordering of peaks and troughs, the correlation between amplitude and phase at each frequency, the arrival sequence of different frequency components, and the non-stationary amplitude envelope shape all remain unchanged by the scaling operation. [@Ref005] Wave propagation effects embedded in the measured record -- including anelastic attenuation, geometric spreading from the blast source, reflections and refractions at geological interfaces, and local site amplification at the measurement point -- are preserved in the sense that those physical effects are implicit in the waveform shape and are not altered by a uniform amplitude multiplier. The source-to-path-to-site transfer function that shaped the record is effectively inherited by the scaled time series; no component of the propagation chain is replaced or re-parameterized. [@Ref001] This inheritance of measured waveform complexity is the principal advantage of scaled measured records over synthetic or modeled waveforms, which require explicit and uncertain parameterization of each physical transfer function.

Several properties are not preserved by amplitude scaling, and each requires explicit declaration. The source-scenario consistency of the scaled record is not guaranteed for large scale factors. When the measured record originates from a blast of specific charge weight, at a specific distance, in a specific geological setting, the amplitude of that record reflects those conditions. Scaling the record to a substantially different amplitude level implies a scenario -- a different charge mass, a different distance, a different source energy, or a different site amplification -- that is not represented in the measured record's source-path-site configuration. Luco and Bazzurro (2007) demonstrate in the earthquake engineering context that scaling records randomly selected from a magnitude-distance bin to a target intensity level introduces bias in nonlinear structural response estimates when the spectral shape of the selected records is not representative of the target scenario; the analogous concern for blast records is that a record scaled far beyond its measured amplitude level may not represent a physically plausible blast waveform for the scenario source and path conditions. [@Ref006] Reporting therefore requires a statement of the measured peak amplitude, the target scenario amplitude, and the resulting scale factor to allow assessment of whether the scaling remains within a physically defensible range.

Inter-component coherency is not preserved when horizontal and vertical components are scaled independently by their respective peak amplitudes. In a measured three-component blast record, the ratio of amplitudes between the two horizontal components and between the horizontal and vertical components encodes information about wave type, source direction, and wave polarization at the measurement point. Applying separate scale factors to each component to equalize or independently target the peak of each component modifies those amplitude ratios and produces a three-component motion vector whose direction and resultant trajectory through time do not correspond to any physical blast source geometry. [@Ref007] This loss of spatial coherency is consequential for multi-dimensional dynamic analysis where the simultaneous excitation of multiple boundaries or the interaction between components produces coupled response; the independently scaled components cannot be interpreted as a physically consistent representation of a single blast event.

Cycle count is preserved by amplitude scaling in the sense that the number of zero-crossings and amplitude peaks in the time series is unchanged. However, the damage-relevant characterization of cumulative cyclic demand in saturated tailings depends not only on the number of cycles but also on the amplitude of each cycle relative to the initial effective vertical stress, expressed through the cyclic stress ratio $CSR = \tau_{cyc} / \sigma_v'$. After amplitude scaling, the amplitude of every cycle scales proportionally, so cycles that were below a critical $CSR$ threshold in the original record may exceed it in the scaled record, and the number of cycles at damaging amplitude levels is therefore a non-linear function of the scale factor. [@Ref008] Pore pressure generation in saturated tailings under cyclic blast loading is sensitive to the amplitude, sequencing, and number of cycles relative to the effective stress state, and is also governed by the constitutive response of the material under undrained cyclic shear; the acceleration time history alone, whether measured or scaled, does not directly determine pore pressure development without a constitutive model and an initial stress state. [@Ref009] Amplitude-scaled blast records consequently do not constitute a direct prediction of cyclic pore pressure generation; they define the kinematic boundary conditions applied to a numerical model, and the pore pressure response is an output of the constitutive calculation.

## SLOT 3: Independent Component-Peak Scaling Assumptions and Limitations

Independent component-peak scaling applies a separate scale factor to each orthogonal acceleration component, with each factor defined as the ratio of the target peak acceleration for that component to the measured peak acceleration of that component. The underlying assumption is that the peak acceleration of each component individually is the controlling demand parameter for the dynamic response quantity of interest, and that simultaneously achieving the target peak on all components produces a bounding or conservative envelope of the loading condition. This assumption is appropriate when the analysis is one-dimensional and the response depends on a single prescribed acceleration component, or when the conservative combination of independently maximized components is an accepted design convention in the applicable regulatory or project framework. [@Ref002] The assumption becomes increasingly restrictive as the analysis becomes multi-dimensional and the coupled response to simultaneous bi- or tri-directional loading depends on the relative phasing and amplitude ratios between components.

The most significant limitation of independent component-peak scaling for TSF dynamic response assessment is the destruction of the physical coherency of the original measured record. The inter-component amplitude ratios in a measured blast waveform are determined by the wave arrival geometry: the relative contributions of compressional and shear wave energy at the measurement point, the source-to-receiver direction, and the instrument orientation relative to the blast. When each component is independently scaled to a target peak, those ratios are replaced by ratios that are solely a consequence of the target amplitude definition, not of any physical wave propagation mechanism. [@Ref005] The resulting three-component input does not represent any single physical blast event at any source configuration; it is instead a multi-component amplitude envelope constructed from the waveform of one blast and the amplitude targets of a defined scenario.

When the scale factors applied to different components differ substantially -- a common outcome when horizontal components in a blast record have different peak accelerations due to source directivity or wave incidence angle -- the independently scaled inputs are conceptually derived from records at different intensity levels, implying that different components represent different implied blast scenarios. A TSF dynamic response analysis using such inputs is therefore a conservative multi-component envelope study rather than a simulation of a single physical loading event. This characterization must be stated explicitly in the analysis methodology, because it affects the interpretation of response quantities that depend on multi-directional loading: rotational displacements, directional deformation patterns, and the spatial distribution of pore pressure generation within the tailings mass are all influenced by the combined action of components and will reflect the artificially maximized inputs rather than any realistic simultaneous loading geometry. [@Ref006][@Ref001]

The declaration for a TSF assessment should further state that pore pressure generation assessed from independently peak-scaled inputs is conditional on the defined amplitude envelope and on the constitutive model parameters of the tailings material, and that the effective stress path followed by the material in the numerical analysis is an artifact of the scaling convention rather than a direct representation of the stress path that would result from the original measured blast event at a different location or intensity. [@Ref009][@Ref008] For reporting, it is further necessary to document the scale factors applied to each component, the measured peak accelerations of the original record, and the target scenario amplitudes, so that an independent reviewer can verify that the scale factors are within a physically plausible range for the blast source and propagation conditions of the measurement campaign. [@Ref006]

## SLOT 4: Distinction from Other Waveform-Adjustment Methods

Amplitude scaling and spectral matching are both methods for adjusting real recorded time series to a target intensity, but they operate on different properties of the record and produce inputs with different characteristics. Amplitude scaling applies a single constant multiplier to the time series, leaving frequency content, phase, and non-stationarity unchanged and modifying only the overall intensity level. Spectral matching adjusts the record so that its acceleration response spectrum conforms to a prescribed target spectrum, typically by adding wavelets to the original time series or by iteratively modifying the Fourier amplitude spectrum of the record. [@Ref004] The consequence of spectral matching is that energy is redistributed across the frequency band: at periods where the record's response spectrum falls below the target, energy is added; at periods where it exceeds the target, energy may be reduced. The resulting waveform no longer has the same Fourier amplitude spectrum as the original record, its non-stationarity is reduced, and the natural record-to-record variability that characterizes the original measurement is artificially suppressed. [@Ref010] For blast vibration records, spectral matching is further constrained by the absence of a standard target response spectrum for near-field blast loading applicable to tailings storage facilities; the definition of such a target would require a separate probabilistic hazard analysis for blast-induced vibration, which is outside the scope of scenario-driven demand history construction using existing measured records. [@Ref003]

Target-spectrum adjustment is the broader methodological category that encompasses spectral matching and any other procedure that modifies a recorded time series to achieve conformance with a defined spectral target across a range of periods. Implementations include frequency-domain amplitude scaling (in which the Fourier amplitude at each frequency is separately scaled to match a target), wavelet addition (in which wavelets are added to the time domain record), and iterative procedures combining both. [@Ref010] All target-spectrum methods share the requirement that a quantitative spectral target be defined before the adjustment can be performed. Amplitude scaling, by contrast, requires only a scalar amplitude target -- a peak acceleration, peak velocity, or other intensity measure -- and does not impose any spectral shape on the adjusted record. This makes amplitude scaling directly applicable to blast scenario studies where the design input is defined by a scenario amplitude (for example, a peak acceleration derived from attenuation modeling at the site boundary) rather than by a site-specific design spectrum. [@Ref002]

Synthetic waveform generation produces acceleration time histories from analytical or stochastic models of the source, propagation medium, and site response, without reference to any specific measured record. Stochastic methods parameterize the source spectral shape using source models, apply distance-dependent path filters, and optionally apply a site transfer function, then generate a time series by sampling a random phase spectrum or by superimposing point-source contributions from a finite-fault model. [@Ref011] The resulting waveform is a model realization whose statistical properties are governed by the chosen parameterization rather than by the physics of any single measured event. For blast vibration sources, stochastic synthesis would require parameterization of the explosive source spectrum, the near-field transmission characteristics of the rock mass, and the site amplification at the analysis boundary -- parameters that require calibration from measured records and introduce model-form uncertainty at each stage. Amplitude-scaled measured records, by contrast, embed the actual propagation physics of the measurement without imposing any model structure on the source or path; all waveform complexity is inherited from the real event, and the only introduced parameter is the scalar scale factor. [@Ref001]

Calibrated blast source modelling develops a site-specific deterministic or semi-empirical model of the explosive source and its near-field propagation, calibrated against a collection of measured blast waveforms. Such a model predicts waveform characteristics at new locations or for new charge configurations by applying the calibrated source-path-site transfer functions to a defined scenario. The predicted waveform is a physics-constrained output of the calibrated model, conditioned on the model parameters and the scenario inputs. This approach differs from amplitude scaling in that the waveform is generated by the model rather than derived from a measured record, and in that the model must be validated against independent measurements before its outputs can be relied upon for engineering design. [@Ref012] For a TSF dynamic response review whose scope is limited to constructing scenario demand histories at specified amplitude levels from an existing measured record archive, a calibrated blast source model would add substantial development and validation effort without necessarily improving the conservatism or defensibility of the resulting demand histories relative to amplitude-scaled measured records. The calibrated model approach is appropriate when the goal is to extrapolate blast vibration predictions to unmeasured locations or charge configurations; amplitude scaling is appropriate when the goal is to adjust the amplitude of a measured event to a scenario level while preserving all other waveform characteristics. [@Ref009]

## SLOT 5: Defensible Wording for Scenario Demand Histories

The acceleration time histories applied in this analysis are amplitude-scaled versions of blast vibration records measured during production blasting at the project site. Each component of the measured three-component acceleration record was multiplied by a scale factor defined as the ratio of the target scenario peak acceleration for that component to the measured peak acceleration of the original record, producing scaled acceleration histories at the defined scenario amplitude level. The resulting time series preserve the frequency content, duration, phase structure, pulse shape, and non-stationary character of the measured event; only the overall amplitude level has been modified. [@Ref002][@Ref005]

These records are described as scenario demand histories in the following sense: the waveform shape, frequency content, and duration are those of the measured blast event, and the peak amplitude has been adjusted to the scenario level by multiplication with the defined scale factor; no assertion is made that a blast event producing exactly these waveforms at the scenario amplitude was measured at the analysis boundary. The source-path-site conditions embedded in the measured records are treated as representative of the scenario loading environment on the basis that the measurement location is proximate to the analysis domain and the blast parameters of the measurement campaign are consistent with the scenario design. [@Ref001] This representativeness assumption is a declaration, not a derived result, and its validity depends on the engineering judgment of the analyst regarding the spatial and parametric consistency of the source-path-site conditions between the measurement and analysis locations.

The horizontal and vertical components of the scaled records were independently scaled to the defined scenario peak acceleration for each component. This procedure maximizes each component at the scenario amplitude level and produces a three-component input whose inter-component amplitude ratios differ from those of the original measured event; the combined input constitutes a conservative amplitude envelope rather than a simulation of a single physical blast geometry. Response quantities that are sensitive to the simultaneous spatial combination of components -- including rotational displacements, directional deformation patterns, and the spatial distribution of shear strain within the tailings mass -- are therefore interpreted as conservative envelope estimates conditional on the independent peak-scaling convention. [@Ref006][@Ref007]

The scaled acceleration histories define the kinematic boundary conditions applied to the numerical model of the tailings storage facility; they do not, by themselves, constitute a prediction of pore pressure development in the saturated tailings. Pore pressure generation under cyclic blast loading is governed by the applied cyclic stress ratio, the number and amplitude of loading cycles, the initial effective stress state, the relative density of the tailings, and the undrained cyclic behavior of the material as represented by the constitutive model. [@Ref009][@Ref008] Any assessment of liquefaction potential or cyclic softening derived from this analysis is therefore conditional on the constitutive model parameters, the initial stress state of the tailings, and the defined scenario amplitude, and must not be interpreted as a prediction based on the acceleration record alone. The acceleration input provides the dynamic loading; the material response is a model output.

For documentation and traceability in the technical report, the recommended wording for the input preparation method is: "Scenario demand histories were derived from blast vibration acceleration records measured at [measurement location] on [date], by amplitude scaling each orthogonal component to a target peak acceleration of [value] m/s$^2$ ([value] g) in the [direction] direction, [value] m/s$^2$ ([value] g) in the [direction] direction, and [value] m/s$^2$ ([value] g) in the vertical direction. The scale factors applied were [SF$_1$], [SF$_2$], and [SF$_3$] for the respective components. The scaled records preserve the frequency content, duration, and phase structure of the original measured event; inter-component amplitude ratios differ from the measured ratios as a result of independent component peak normalization, and the combined three-component input is treated as a conservative amplitude envelope. Pore pressure response to this loading is evaluated using the constitutive model of the saturated tailings and is conditional on the model parameters and scenario amplitude definition." [@Ref002][@Ref001]
