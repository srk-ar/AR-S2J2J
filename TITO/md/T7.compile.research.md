### SLOT 1: Support for amplitude-scaling measured records

The strongest support for amplitude-scaling measured records comes from geotechnical earthquake engineering and transient response-history practice rather than from blast-specific standards alone. NIST guidance on response-history analysis explicitly treats ground-motion selection, generating, scaling, and response-spectrum matching as distinct topics for performance-based seismic engineering, which supports the general practice of using recorded histories after controlled amplitude adjustment when the objective is scenario response assessment rather than reconstruction of a new source event. [@Ref001] A recent seismological review describes accelerogram amplitude scaling as multiplication of a recorded accelerogram by a positive scale factor to intensify amplitudes without changing duration or frequency content, and it identifies amplitude scaling as a common engineering practice used because extreme recorded motions are scarce. [@Ref002] For the TSF application, this literature supports amplitude scaling as a transparent scenario-construction operation on measured demand histories, provided that the report states the scale factor, target amplitude measure, component treatment, and limits on physical interpretation.

Blast-vibration standards and guidance provide more direct support for measurement, documentation, and peak-vibration interpretation than for unrestricted waveform rescaling. The KB synthesis identifies ISO 4866, BS 7385-2, BS 6472-2, U.S. surface-mining blast rules, IFC mining guidance, and the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management as relevant context for measuring and reporting blast vibration, separating structural damage criteria from human exposure criteria, and treating TSF performance as a distinct geotechnical question. [@Ref003] That evidence supports using measured blast records as the empirical basis for scenario histories because their timing, pulse shape, instrumentation characteristics, and site-specific waveform features are real measured quantities, but it does not by itself prove that a scaled blast record represents a physically realized blast at a different charge, distance, delay pattern, or site condition. [@Ref003][@Ref002]

### SLOT 2: Preserved and non-preserved properties

For a single component scaled as $a_s(t) = c a(t)$, simple amplitude scaling preserves the time axis, record duration, relative timing of peaks, waveform polarity, phase timing within the component, normalized pulse shape, and frequency locations in the Fourier description; it changes the amplitudes of the acceleration history and any linearly derived velocity, displacement, Fourier amplitude, or linear-response spectrum by the same scale factor when processing is otherwise unchanged. [@Ref002] If the same scale factor is applied to all measured components, the component timing, relative component amplitudes, and vector particle-motion geometry are also preserved apart from the common amplitude multiplier; if each component is assigned its own scale factor, each component retains its own internal waveform shape, but the original component amplitude ratios, vector orientation, polarization, and amplitude-based coherency are no longer preserved as a measured three-component vector field. [@Ref002]

Simple amplitude scaling does not create new duration, add wave cycles, modify arrival sequence, shift frequency content, alter phase delays, or encode changed wave propagation, charge geometry, delay scatter, attenuation, scattering, topography, or site-response effects. [@Ref002][@Ref003] It therefore preserves the measured cycle count in the waveform but does not preserve pore-pressure-relevant cumulative demand in saturated tailings, because pore pressure generation depends on stress or strain history, density and state, drainage conditions, constitutive response, and previous cyclic loading rather than on acceleration amplitude alone. [@Ref004][@Ref005] In a dynamic TSF model, the scaled acceleration record can define an imposed kinematic demand history, but any excess pore-pressure response has to come from the coupled soil model, material calibration, drainage assumptions, initial state, and effective-stress calculation rather than from the acceleration trace by direct conversion. [@Ref004][@Ref006]

### SLOT 3: Independent component-peak scaling assumptions and limitations

When measured blast records are scaled independently by component peaks, the method assumes that the design or scenario target for each component can be represented by multiplying that component by its own scalar factor and that the time history within each component remains a useful empirical waveform for dynamic input. [@Ref002] A defensible description states that the method targets specified component peak values, retains the measured component timing and waveform shape within each channel, and does not perform spectral matching, synthetic waveform generation, or calibrated source-path-site simulation. [@Ref001][@Ref006] The method documentation identifies the reference record, instrument location, orientation convention, baseline or filtering status, target peak quantity, component scale factors, sign convention, and whether the applied input is acceleration, velocity, displacement, or a processed derivative of the measured record. [@Ref003][@Ref006]

The principal limitation is that independently scaled component peaks no longer form a measured three-component vector record in the strict sense, even though each channel still has a measured waveform shape. Component-to-component peak ratios, resultant particle-motion envelopes, amplitude-dependent coherency, and directional demand can be changed by the chosen target peaks, so interpretations based on vector direction, combined horizontal demand, or simultaneous peak resultant are labelled as scenario assumptions. [@Ref002] For TSF dynamic response assessment, the method is declared as an input-demand construction for sensitivity or scenario review, not as evidence that a blast with the same source mechanism, charge distribution, delay pattern, path attenuation, and site response occurred or occurs under a future scenario. [@Ref003][@Ref002]

### SLOT 4: Distinction from other waveform-adjustment methods

Amplitude scaling is the least invasive waveform-adjustment method considered here: it multiplies a measured record by one or more scalar factors and leaves the time axis, phase timing, duration, and frequency locations of the measured trace unchanged. [@Ref002] Spectral matching and target-spectrum adjustment have a different objective because they modify a record so that its response spectrum better conforms to a target spectrum; NIST treats response-spectrum matching as a distinct ground-motion topic alongside selection, generation, and scaling, so a report based only on scalar multiplication of measured blast records is not described as spectrum-matched or target-spectrum-compatible unless that additional operation was actually performed. [@Ref001] EEMD or other filtering is also distinct because it decomposes and reconstructs scale bands in the measured signal and can alter peak values, baseline behavior, frequency content, and response spectra depending on component-selection choices. [@Ref006]

Synthetic waveform generation and calibrated blast source modelling are more ambitious than amplitude scaling because they attempt to create or simulate a waveform from model assumptions, statistical training data, source mechanics, propagation, site terms, or calibration records. Data-driven accelerogram synthesis work, for example, describes generating three-component accelerograms conditioned on variables such as magnitude, distance, and $V_{s30}$, while comparative synthetic-versus-recorded studies treat generated motions as a different input class from recorded motions. [@Ref007][@Ref008] A calibrated blast source model requires explicit representation or empirical calibration of blast layout, charge per delay, timing, source radiation, path attenuation, and local site response, whereas simple amplitude scaling keeps the measured waveform and changes only its imposed amplitude level. [@Ref003][@Ref002]

### SLOT 5: Defensible wording for scenario demand histories

Defensible report wording states that measured blast vibration records were used as empirical waveform templates and were amplitude-scaled to the selected scenario peak demands for TSF dynamic response review. The scaled histories are described as scenario demand histories or scaled measured input motions, not as measured events at the scenario amplitude and not as synthetic predictions of a future blast. [@Ref002][@Ref003] The following formulation is defensible: "The input histories are measured blast records multiplied by documented component scale factors to match the adopted scenario peak amplitudes. The scaling preserves the measured timing and waveform shape within each component, while the scaled amplitudes represent scenario demand levels for analysis rather than a calibrated reconstruction of source, path, or site effects." [@Ref002]

For saturated-tailings response, the report language keeps the acceleration input and pore-pressure response separate. A defensible statement is: "The scaled acceleration histories were applied as external dynamic demand histories to the TSF response model. Excess pore pressure, strain accumulation, deformation, and stability response are model outputs conditional on the selected constitutive model, material parameters, initial effective stresses, drainage assumptions, and loading history; they are not inferred directly from the scaled acceleration records alone." [@Ref004][@Ref005] Where components are scaled independently, an additional limitation reads: "Independent component scaling matches the selected component peak demands but may alter the measured vector amplitude ratios and directional particle-motion characteristics, so vector-resultant response measures are interpreted as scenario responses under the stated scaling convention." [@Ref002]
