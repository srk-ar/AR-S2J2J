# EEMD Filtering and Reconstruction: Claim Support, Limitations, and Validation

### SLOT 1: Authoritative EMD/EEMD Filtering References

The most authoritative starting point for EMD-based filtering claims in engineering vibration applications is Huang et al. (1998), which introduced empirical mode decomposition as the decomposition stage of the Hilbert-Huang transform for nonlinear and non-stationary records and established the intrinsic mode function and residual reconstruction framework used by all later filtering studies. [@Ref001][@Ref001][@Ref002] That source supports EMD as an adaptive decomposition of a measured record, but it does not by itself justify treating any retained intrinsic mode function as a uniquely physical engineering component.

The central EEMD reference is Wu and Huang (2009), which defines ensemble empirical mode decomposition as a noise-assisted method in which finite-amplitude white noise is added over repeated trials and ensemble averaging is used to reduce mode mixing and obtain a scale-organized decomposition. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref003] Flandrin, Rilling, and Goncalves (2004) and Wu and Huang (2004) are also authoritative for filtering claims because they connect EMD behavior to filter-bank-like and noise-scale behavior, which is the main basis for describing intrinsic mode functions as adaptive scale bands rather than preassigned physical modes. [@Ref001][@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref004][@Ref005]

For engineering filtering rather than basic decomposition, Boudraa and Cexus (2007) and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) are more directly relevant because they treat EMD as a signal filtering or denoising tool and address component selection or thresholding in measurement and signal-processing contexts. [@Ref001][@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref006][@Ref007] Huang and Wu (2008) serves as a useful review source because it summarizes Hilbert-Huang transform applications in geophysical records and discusses open methodological problems, making it appropriate for limitation language in an engineering report. [@Ref001][@Ref007][@Ref008]

For transient acceleration histories, the EMD and EEMD sources are best paired with strong-motion processing references. Boore and Bommer (2005) documents the consequences of filtering, baseline correction, and filter choices on accelerograms and response spectra, providing the primary framework for evaluating whether any adaptive filtering preserves engineering-relevant signal content. [@Ref001][@Ref008][@Ref009] Boore (2005) addresses pad and filter processing of strong-motion data and is particularly relevant to end-effect and integration behavior. [@Ref001][@Ref009][@Ref010] Boore and Akkar (2003) documents how causal and acausal filter choices affect elastic and inelastic response spectra derived from processed accelerograms, demonstrating that spectral consequences of filtering can be significant even at periods well below the filter corner frequency. [@Ref001][@Ref010][@Ref011]

### SLOT 2: Supported Component Removal Practices

The literature supports component removal in EMD and EEMD only as a documented filtering or detrending operation followed by reconstruction, not as automatic evidence that the removed or retained intrinsic mode functions are physically unique blast-wave components. [@Ref001][@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref003] EMD and EEMD decompositions are adaptive and reconstructive, so a processed history can be formed by summing selected intrinsic mode functions and, when appropriate, a residual term; however, the selection criterion must be stated as an engineering or signal-processing rule, such as removal of components dominated by high-frequency noise, removal of a low-frequency trend, or retention of components inside a target response band. A defensible report therefore presents intrinsic mode function removal as scale-selective filtering based on evidence from the record, not as a decomposition into uniquely named source mechanisms. [@Ref001][@Ref007][@Ref008]

Removal of early, high-frequency intrinsic mode functions is supported when those components are shown to be dominated by measurement noise or by frequencies outside the engineering response range of interest. Boudraa and Cexus (2007) supports EMD-based signal filtering using partial reconstruction from selected intrinsic mode functions, and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) supports denoising approaches that treat intrinsic mode function content with thresholding logic rather than a blanket rule that the first intrinsic mode function is always noise. [@Ref001][@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref006][@Ref007] Removal of a residual or trend term can be defensible when the residual represents baseline drift or nonphysical low-frequency contamination, but the same operation can also remove real long-period motion; in acceleration-history applications, such removal must therefore be treated as a high-pass or trend-removal choice whose consequences are checked in integrated velocity, displacement, and response spectra. [@Ref001][@Ref008][@Ref009][@Ref010][@Ref009][@Ref010][@Ref011]

Selection of nonadjacent or middle intrinsic mode functions is supported only when the selection is tied to a stated diagnostic, such as frequency content, energy contribution, correlation with the target signal, response-spectral relevance, or comparison with independent instrumentation or physical modeling. [@Ref001][@Ref011][@Ref012] Rilling and Flandrin (2008) showed that EMD separation of nearby frequency content depends on signal conditions, so component selection cannot be assumed to isolate every true modal contribution in a short transient record. [@Ref001][@Ref011][@Ref012] EEMD reduces mode mixing by adding noise-assisted ensemble averaging, but the method still depends on ensemble size, added-noise amplitude, sifting choices, and endpoint treatment, so retained intrinsic mode function groups are better described as selected scale bands than as verified physical modes. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref007][@Ref003][@Ref008]

### SLOT 3: Known Limitations To Report

Mode mixing is a primary limitation to report for any EEMD-filtered engineering history. EEMD was proposed specifically to reduce the tendency of EMD to place disparate scales in the same intrinsic mode function or split a similar scale across several intrinsic mode functions, but its remedy uses added white noise and ensemble averaging rather than a deterministic physical separation. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref003] The reported processing must therefore identify the added-noise level, ensemble size, sifting or stopping criterion, randomization policy where relevant, and any post-processing or alignment method used to compare intrinsic mode functions across trials. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref007][@Ref003][@Ref008]

End effects, non-uniqueness, and parameter sensitivity are also material limitations that must be disclosed in any report using EEMD-filtered records. EMD envelopes are built from local extrema, so behavior near the beginning and end of a short record can be sensitive to interpolation and boundary extension choices; this is especially important for transient vibration histories in which the main pulse, decay tail, or baseline segment may occur near a boundary. [@Ref001][@Ref001][@Ref007][@Ref002][@Ref008] The decomposition is not a fixed orthogonal transform with a predeclared basis, and different sifting, stopping, noise, and endpoint choices can change the intrinsic mode function set; therefore the same measured history can yield different but plausible component partitions, which means that reported results are conditional on the stated parameter set. [@Ref001][@Ref003][@Ref002][@Ref004][@Ref003]

Loss of physical interpretability is a limitation whenever intrinsic mode functions are used for report-level engineering claims. EMD and EEMD components are derived from local time-scale behavior, so the existence of an intrinsic mode function group does not by itself demonstrate a separate blast source, wave path, soil layer response, sensor artifact, or structural mode. [@Ref001][@Ref001][@Ref011][@Ref002][@Ref012] Filtering can also alter peak values, apparent pulse duration, low-frequency drift, and response demands; those consequences are well documented in the strong-motion filtering and baseline processing literature and are relevant when processed acceleration histories are used downstream in geotechnical or structural assessment. [@Ref001][@Ref008][@Ref010][@Ref009][@Ref011]

### SLOT 4: Validation Checks For EEMD-Filtered Histories

Validation of an EEMD-filtered acceleration history is strongest when it compares the raw record, the retained reconstruction, and the removed residual content in both time and frequency domains. Peak preservation checks include the signed and absolute peak acceleration, peak timing, pulse polarity, and local pulse shape near the peak, because a filter that improves visual smoothness can still alter the controlling demand in a transient history. [@Ref001][@Ref008][@Ref009][@Ref009][@Ref010] Baseline and drift checks include numerical integration to velocity and displacement, comparison of terminal velocity and displacement trends, and review of any low-frequency residual removed from the reconstruction. [@Ref001][@Ref008][@Ref009]

Energy and frequency-content checks are defensible because EMD filtering is scale-selective. Comparison of root-mean-square acceleration, summed squared acceleration, cumulative signal energy, Fourier or power spectral density estimates, Hilbert marginal spectra where used, and the frequency content of removed intrinsic mode functions confirms that the operation removed the intended scale range rather than suppressing engineering demand. [@Ref001][@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref004][@Ref006][@Ref007][@Ref005] Response-spectrum checks are important when the processed acceleration is used as an engineering input, because response spectra can be sensitive to filtering choices and the difference may be consequential even when the acceleration trace appears acceptable in the time domain. [@Ref001][@Ref010][@Ref008][@Ref011][@Ref009]

Repeatability and visual diagnostics are also necessary for EEMD-filtered histories. Because EEMD depends on added-noise amplitude and ensemble averaging, the filtered result can be checked by rerunning the decomposition with the same documented parameters and, where relevant, with alternative random seeds or nearby ensemble settings to confirm that retained scale bands and validation metrics are stable under parameter variation. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref007][@Ref003][@Ref008] Visual review includes overlaid raw and filtered acceleration traces, removed-component traces, endpoint behavior, pre-event or post-event baseline segments when available, and plots of cumulative residuals or integrated histories, so that the report demonstrates whether the reconstruction preserves the features that control engineering response. [@Ref001][@Ref008][@Ref009][@Ref009][@Ref010]

### SLOT 5: Description Without Overclaiming

EEMD filtering is appropriately described in an engineering report as an adaptive, data-driven filtering and reconstruction procedure applied to a measured vibration history. A conservative description states that selected intrinsic mode functions were retained or removed according to documented engineering criteria, and that the resulting reconstruction was accepted only after checks on peak response, energy, frequency content, baseline behavior, and response spectra. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref005][@Ref008][@Ref003][@Ref006][@Ref009] The description avoids stating that retained modes are physically unique blast-wave components unless separate evidence from instrumentation, site physics, wave-propagation modeling, or independent measurements supports that interpretation. [@Ref001][@Ref001][@Ref011][@Ref002][@Ref012]

Appropriate terminology for report use includes "retained intrinsic mode function group," "removed scale band," "EEMD-filtered reconstruction," "adaptive denoising," and "trend or residual removal," because those terms describe the processing operation without asserting a source mechanism. [@Ref001][@Ref003][@Ref006][@Ref004][@Ref007] Terms such as "true blast component," "physical mode," or "isolated wave type" require additional support and are not justified by the decomposition alone, because EMD separation depends on signal conditions and EEMD depends on noise-assisted ensemble choices. [@Ref001][@Ref011][@Ref002][@Ref012][@Ref003]

A publication-ready limitation statement can note that EEMD was used to reduce unwanted scale content while preserving defined engineering measures, and that the filtered record is a processed representation of the measured history rather than a recovered unique physical waveform. [@Ref001][@Ref005][@Ref008][@Ref006][@Ref009] The report can further state that conclusions based on the filtered history are conditional on the documented EEMD parameters, component-selection rule, and validation results, and that raw and filtered histories remain distinguishable in the record archive and in subsequent calculations. [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref007][@Ref003][@Ref008]
