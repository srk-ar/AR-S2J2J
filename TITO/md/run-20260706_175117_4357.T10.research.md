## SLOT 1: Support for Amplitude-Scaling Measured Records

The use of real recorded ground motion time series, adjusted to a target amplitude level by multiplication with a scalar factor, is a well-established procedure in geotechnical and structural dynamic analysis. Bommer and Acevedo (2004) provide a comprehensive treatment of the use of real accelerograms as input to dynamic analyses, documenting amplitude scaling as a permissible and widely applied adjustment method that retains the duration, frequency content, phase structure, and non-stationary character of the original measurement while proportionally modifying its intensity level. [WEB:https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-USE-OF-REAL-EARTHQUAKE-ACCELEROGRAMS-AS-INPUT-Bommer-Acevedo/84f53edfeb3a19a7e9601645ac5bfc09795ec228] The NIST GCR 11-917-15 report on selecting and scaling earthquake ground motions for nonlinear dynamic analysis formalizes amplitude scaling as a standard component of the ground motion preparation workflow, defining it explicitly as multiplication of the recorded acceleration time series by a constant scalar, and establishes guidance on the selection of records and the application of scale factors in a way that preserves the physical character of the original waveform. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Both cited sources are authoritative references in the ground motion selection and scaling literature. Bommer and Acevedo (2004) is a peer-reviewed journal review article covering the full range of record selection and adjustment practices, and NIST GCR 11-917-15 is a government-sponsored technical document widely used in structural and geotechnical seismic analysis. The claims attributed to these sources are consistent with the well-established definition of amplitude scaling and have been independently confirmed by multiple additional sources found during the external research pass.]

For blast vibration records, the rationale for amplitude scaling is grounded in the same physical principle that underlies earthquake record scaling: a measured blast vibration record contains the combined signature of the explosive source, the transmission path through rock and soil, and the site response at the measurement location. When the engineering objective is to define a scenario demand history at a specified amplitude level, multiplying the measured record by a scalar preserves all of these waveform characteristics while adjusting only the overall intensity. This approach is consistent with the measurement and reporting framework described in blast vibration standards, which characterize measured records in terms of peak particle velocity, waveform attributes, and distance-and-charge-dependent attenuation, treating the recorded waveform as the physical output of a specific source-path-site combination. [KB:T1.compile.research.md] The transient, non-stationary character of blast acceleration histories -- with a rapid rise, a dominant pulse region, and a decaying tail -- is preserved through amplitude scaling in a manner that is not replicable by idealized harmonic or sinusoidal waveforms of equivalent amplitude.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical reasoning is well supported: amplitude scaling preserves the waveform shape by construction, as the operation is a linear time-invariant multiplication. The characterization of blast records as source-path-site-dependent waveforms is consistent with KB:T1.compile.research.md, which documents that measured PPV and waveform attributes reflect the specific source-path-site configuration. The extrapolation from earthquake record scaling practice to blast record scaling is engineering-reasonable and supported by the ATCWilliams dam analysis reference, though direct peer-reviewed literature addressing blast record amplitude scaling for TSF dynamic analysis is limited; the cited sources establish the broader methodological basis.]

In dam and embankment geotechnical analysis, amplitude scaling of measured seed records is accepted as one of the two principal methods for preparing ground motion inputs, the other being spectral matching. The distinction between them, and the specific advantages of amplitude scaling for preserving natural record variability, are described in the dam-specific guidance literature as follows: linear scaling applies a constant time-domain factor so that all waveform characteristics except amplitude are unchanged, whereas spectral matching modifies the frequency content and energy distribution of the record. [WEB:https://atcwilliams.com/appropriate-earthquake-loads-for-design-of-embankment-dams/] For a tailings storage facility dynamic response review in which the objective is to construct scenario demand histories at defined amplitude levels from existing measured blast records, amplitude scaling is the method that most directly achieves that objective: it adjusts the scenario amplitude without altering the waveform structure of the measured event, and it requires no spectral target that does not exist for near-field blast loading. The blasting and geotechnical transient dynamics literature supports the broader principle that measured records scaled to scenario amplitudes constitute a defensible basis for dynamic response assessment when the source-path-site conditions of the measured event are representative of the scenario loading environment. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf][WEB:https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-USE-OF-REAL-EARTHQUAKE-ACCELEROGRAMS-AS-INPUT-Bommer-Acevedo/84f53edfeb3a19a7e9601645ac5bfc09795ec228]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The ATCWilliams source supports amplitude scaling for embankment dam analysis, and NIST GCR 11-917-15 supports it for general nonlinear dynamic analysis. However, the specific application to blast vibration records as inputs to TSF dynamic response review, as distinct from earthquake records for structural analysis, is not directly addressed in a single authoritative peer-reviewed reference; the claim is an engineering extrapolation from the established earthquake engineering literature to the blast domain, which is reasonable given the identical physics of the scaling operation but warrants declaration as an assumption in report wording.]

## SLOT 2: Preserved and Non-Preserved Properties

Amplitude scaling is defined by the operation $a_s(t) = SF \cdot a(t)$, where $a(t)$ is the measured acceleration time series and $SF$ is a constant scalar scale factor. Because the scale factor is constant in time and does not depend on frequency, the operation is linear and time-invariant. The Fourier transform of the scaled record $A_s(f) = SF \cdot A(f)$ preserves both the shape of the Fourier amplitude spectrum and the Fourier phase spectrum; only the absolute amplitude at each frequency changes, by the same factor $SF$. As a direct consequence, the normalized spectral shape -- the ratio of amplitude at any frequency to the amplitude at any reference frequency -- is unchanged. [ARXIV:2305.05631] Duration metrics that depend on relative amplitude thresholds are also preserved: the bracketed duration, defined by the interval between threshold crossings at a fixed fraction of peak amplitude, is unchanged because the fraction is maintained after scaling. The significant duration $D_{5-95}$, defined as the interval over which the Arias intensity accumulates from five to ninety-five percent of its total, is equally preserved, because the Arias intensity scales uniformly by $SF^2$ (each squared amplitude sample in the accumulation scales by the same factor), and the percentile times that define $D_{5-95}$ are determined by the shape of the normalized cumulative energy curve rather than its absolute level; that shape is identical in the original and scaled records. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The preservation of frequency content and phase by amplitude scaling is a mathematical identity, confirmed explicitly in ARXIV:2305.05631 and consistent with the NIST GCR 11-917-15 framework. The derivation of duration preservation from the proportional scaling of the Arias intensity is internally consistent and standard in the ground motion scaling literature. No source contradiction was found.]

Phase relationships within the record are fully preserved. The temporal ordering of peaks and troughs, the correlation between amplitude and phase at each frequency, the arrival sequence of different frequency components, and the non-stationary amplitude envelope shape all remain unchanged by the scaling operation. [ARXIV:2305.05631] Wave propagation effects embedded in the measured record -- including anelastic attenuation, geometric spreading from the blast source, reflections and refractions at geological interfaces, and local site amplification at the measurement point -- are preserved in the sense that those physical effects are implicit in the waveform shape and are not altered by a uniform amplitude multiplier. The source-to-path-to-site transfer function that shaped the record is effectively inherited by the scaled time series; no component of the propagation chain is replaced or re-parameterized. [WEB:https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-USE-OF-REAL-EARTHQUAKE-ACCELEROGRAMS-AS-INPUT-Bommer-Acevedo/84f53edfeb3a19a7e9601645ac5bfc09795ec228] This inheritance of measured waveform complexity is the principal advantage of scaled measured records over synthetic or modeled waveforms, which require explicit and uncertain parameterization of each physical transfer function.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The preservation of phase relationships is a direct consequence of the definition of amplitude scaling and is confirmed in ARXIV:2305.05631. The argument that wave propagation effects are embedded in the measured waveform and therefore preserved by a uniform multiplier is physically sound and consistent with Bommer and Acevedo (2004), who identify this as the key motivation for using real records rather than synthetics. The claims are well supported and internally consistent.]

Several properties are not preserved by amplitude scaling, and each requires explicit declaration. The source-scenario consistency of the scaled record is not guaranteed for large scale factors. When the measured record originates from a blast of specific charge weight, at a specific distance, in a specific geological setting, the amplitude of that record reflects those conditions. Scaling the record to a substantially different amplitude level implies a scenario -- a different charge mass, a different distance, a different source energy, or a different site amplification -- that is not represented in the measured record's source-path-site configuration. Luco and Bazzurro (2007) demonstrate in the earthquake engineering context that scaling records randomly selected from a magnitude-distance bin to a target intensity level introduces bias in nonlinear structural response estimates when the spectral shape of the selected records is not representative of the target scenario; the analogous concern for blast records is that a record scaled far beyond its measured amplitude level may not represent a physically plausible blast waveform for the scenario source and path conditions. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.695] Reporting therefore requires a statement of the measured peak amplitude, the target scenario amplitude, and the resulting scale factor to allow assessment of whether the scaling remains within a physically defensible range.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Luco and Bazzurro (2007) is a peer-reviewed journal article (DOI:10.1002/eqe.695) providing direct evidence of bias from amplitude scaling when source-scenario consistency is not maintained. The extension of this principle from earthquake records to blast records is an engineering extrapolation supported by the identical physics of the scaling operation. The characterization of the concern and the reporting requirement is consistent with Bommer and Acevedo (2004).]

Inter-component coherency is not preserved when horizontal and vertical components are scaled independently by their respective peak amplitudes. In a measured three-component blast record, the ratio of amplitudes between the two horizontal components and between the horizontal and vertical components encodes information about wave type, source direction, and wave polarization at the measurement point. Applying separate scale factors to each component to equalize or independently target the peak of each component modifies those amplitude ratios and produces a three-component motion vector whose direction and resultant trajectory through time do not correspond to any physical blast source geometry. [WEB:https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0186.pdf] This loss of spatial coherency is consequential for multi-dimensional dynamic analysis where the simultaneous excitation of multiple boundaries or the interaction between components produces coupled response; the independently scaled components cannot be interpreted as a physically consistent representation of a single blast event.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The importance of preserving inter-component amplitude ratios and the consequences of independent component scaling for multi-dimensional analysis are well established in the ground motion selection literature. The WCEE paper provides direct support, and the argument follows logically from the physics of wave polarization. NIST GCR 11-917-15 discusses component scaling criteria in the multi-directional analysis context consistently with this claim.]

Cycle count is preserved by amplitude scaling in the sense that the number of zero-crossings and amplitude peaks in the time series is unchanged. However, the damage-relevant characterization of cumulative cyclic demand in saturated tailings depends not only on the number of cycles but also on the amplitude of each cycle relative to the initial effective vertical stress, expressed through the cyclic stress ratio $CSR = \tau_{cyc} / \sigma_v'$. After amplitude scaling, the amplitude of every cycle scales proportionally, so cycles that were below a critical $CSR$ threshold in the original record may exceed it in the scaled record, and the number of cycles at damaging amplitude levels is therefore a non-linear function of the scale factor. [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337801086_Analysis_and_Seismic_Design_of_Tailings_Dams_and_Liquefaction_Assessment] Pore pressure generation in saturated tailings under cyclic blast loading is sensitive to the amplitude, sequencing, and number of cycles relative to the effective stress state, and is also governed by the constitutive response of the material under undrained cyclic shear; the acceleration time history alone, whether measured or scaled, does not directly determine pore pressure development without a constitutive model and an initial stress state. [KB:T3.compile.research.md] Amplitude-scaled blast records consequently do not constitute a direct prediction of cyclic pore pressure generation; they define the kinematic boundary conditions applied to a numerical model, and the pore pressure response is an output of the constitutive calculation.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The multi-parameter nature of pore pressure generation under cyclic loading in saturated tailings is supported by the tailings seismic paper and is consistent with the framework described in KB:T3.compile.research.md regarding the role of constitutive models in interpreting processed acceleration histories. The reasoning that cycle count is preserved but the amplitude-dependent demand profile changes non-linearly with the scale factor is internally consistent and follows directly from the definition of CSR-based liquefaction analysis. The constraint in the task file that no direct pore-pressure prediction from records alone may be claimed is consistent with this finding.]

## SLOT 3: Independent Component-Peak Scaling Assumptions and Limitations

Independent component-peak scaling applies a separate scale factor to each orthogonal acceleration component, with each factor defined as the ratio of the target peak acceleration for that component to the measured peak acceleration of that component. The underlying assumption is that the peak acceleration of each component individually is the controlling demand parameter for the dynamic response quantity of interest, and that simultaneously achieving the target peak on all components produces a bounding or conservative envelope of the loading condition. This assumption is appropriate when the analysis is one-dimensional and the response depends on a single prescribed acceleration component, or when the conservative combination of independently maximized components is an accepted design convention in the applicable regulatory or project framework. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf] The assumption becomes increasingly restrictive as the analysis becomes multi-dimensional and the coupled response to simultaneous bi- or tri-directional loading depends on the relative phasing and amplitude ratios between components.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: NIST GCR 11-917-15 discusses single-component and multi-component scaling procedures and their respective applicability conditions. The characterization of independent peak scaling as a conservative envelope convention for one-dimensional analysis is consistent with standard geotechnical analysis practice. The limitation for multi-dimensional coupled analysis is well established in the ground motion selection literature and follows logically from the loss of inter-component coherency documented in Slot 2.]

The most significant limitation of independent component-peak scaling for TSF dynamic response assessment is the destruction of the physical coherency of the original measured record. The inter-component amplitude ratios in a measured blast waveform are determined by the wave arrival geometry: the relative contributions of compressional and shear wave energy at the measurement point, the source-to-receiver direction, and the instrument orientation relative to the blast. When each component is independently scaled to a target peak, those ratios are replaced by ratios that are solely a consequence of the target amplitude definition, not of any physical wave propagation mechanism. [ARXIV:2305.05631] The resulting three-component input does not represent any single physical blast event at any source configuration; it is instead a multi-component amplitude envelope constructed from the waveform of one blast and the amplitude targets of a defined scenario.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The destruction of inter-component coherency by independent peak scaling is a direct mathematical consequence of applying different scale factors to each component, confirmed by the wave physics reasoning in ARXIV:2305.05631 and the ground motion selection literature discussion of component scaling. The claim is internally consistent with the properties reviewed in Slot 2 and does not rely on any single-source assertion.]

When the scale factors applied to different components differ substantially -- a common outcome when horizontal components in a blast record have different peak accelerations due to source directivity or wave incidence angle -- the independently scaled inputs are conceptually derived from records at different intensity levels, implying that different components represent different implied blast scenarios. A TSF dynamic response analysis using such inputs is therefore a conservative multi-component envelope study rather than a simulation of a single physical loading event. This characterization must be stated explicitly in the analysis methodology, because it affects the interpretation of response quantities that depend on multi-directional loading: rotational displacements, directional deformation patterns, and the spatial distribution of pore pressure generation within the tailings mass are all influenced by the combined action of components and will reflect the artificially maximized inputs rather than any realistic simultaneous loading geometry. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.695][WEB:https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-USE-OF-REAL-EARTHQUAKE-ACCELEROGRAMS-AS-INPUT-Bommer-Acevedo/84f53edfeb3a19a7e9601645ac5bfc09795ec228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Luco and Bazzurro (2007) and Bommer and Acevedo (2004) both address the implications of scenario consistency and the interpretation of scaling-induced biases in structural and geotechnical response. The characterization of independently scaled multi-component inputs as conservative multi-envelope inputs rather than physical event simulations is consistent with the established treatment of this issue in the ground motion preparation literature.]

The declaration for a TSF assessment should further state that pore pressure generation assessed from independently peak-scaled inputs is conditional on the defined amplitude envelope and on the constitutive model parameters of the tailings material, and that the effective stress path followed by the material in the numerical analysis is an artifact of the scaling convention rather than a direct representation of the stress path that would result from the original measured blast event at a different location or intensity. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337801086_Analysis_and_Seismic_Design_of_Tailings_Dams_and_Liquefaction_Assessment] For reporting, it is further necessary to document the scale factors applied to each component, the measured peak accelerations of the original record, and the target scenario amplitudes, so that an independent reviewer can verify that the scale factors are within a physically plausible range for the blast source and propagation conditions of the measurement campaign. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.695]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The requirement to report scale factors and measured peaks for traceability is supported by Luco and Bazzurro (2007) and NIST GCR 11-917-15. The conditional interpretation of pore pressure results with respect to constitutive model assumptions is supported by the tailings seismic literature and KB:T3. No source contradictions were detected; claims are consistent across cited sources.]

## SLOT 4: Distinction from Other Waveform-Adjustment Methods

Amplitude scaling and spectral matching are both methods for adjusting real recorded time series to a target intensity, but they operate on different properties of the record and produce inputs with different characteristics. Amplitude scaling applies a single constant multiplier to the time series, leaving frequency content, phase, and non-stationarity unchanged and modifying only the overall intensity level. Spectral matching adjusts the record so that its acceleration response spectrum conforms to a prescribed target spectrum, typically by adding wavelets to the original time series or by iteratively modifying the Fourier amplitude spectrum of the record. [WEB:https://atcwilliams.com/appropriate-earthquake-loads-for-design-of-embankment-dams/] The consequence of spectral matching is that energy is redistributed across the frequency band: at periods where the record's response spectrum falls below the target, energy is added; at periods where it exceeds the target, energy may be reduced. The resulting waveform no longer has the same Fourier amplitude spectrum as the original record, its non-stationarity is reduced, and the natural record-to-record variability that characterizes the original measurement is artificially suppressed. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0] For blast vibration records, spectral matching is further constrained by the absence of a standard target response spectrum for near-field blast loading applicable to tailings storage facilities; the definition of such a target would require a separate probabilistic hazard analysis for blast-induced vibration, which is outside the scope of scenario-driven demand history construction using existing measured records. [KB:T1.compile.research.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between amplitude scaling and spectral matching is well established in the ground motion preparation literature and is confirmed by both the ATCWilliams dam analysis source and the spectral matching selection paper from the Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering. The characterization of spectral matching's effect on frequency content and non-stationarity is consistent with multiple sources. The claim that no standard target spectrum for blast vibration loading of TSF structures exists is supported by the absence of such a target in the cited blast vibration standards (KB:T1) and is consistent with the scope of the task.]

Target-spectrum adjustment is the broader methodological category that encompasses spectral matching and any other procedure that modifies a recorded time series to achieve conformance with a defined spectral target across a range of periods. Implementations include frequency-domain amplitude scaling (in which the Fourier amplitude at each frequency is separately scaled to match a target), wavelet addition (in which wavelets are added to the time domain record), and iterative procedures combining both. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0] All target-spectrum methods share the requirement that a quantitative spectral target be defined before the adjustment can be performed. Amplitude scaling, by contrast, requires only a scalar amplitude target -- a peak acceleration, peak velocity, or other intensity measure -- and does not impose any spectral shape on the adjusted record. This makes amplitude scaling directly applicable to blast scenario studies where the design input is defined by a scenario amplitude (for example, a peak acceleration derived from attenuation modeling at the site boundary) rather than by a site-specific design spectrum. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of target-spectrum adjustment methods and their implementations is well documented in the ground motion selection literature, including the Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering paper and NIST GCR 11-917-15. The characterization of amplitude scaling as requiring only a scalar target rather than a spectral shape is a definitional fact that is consistent across all sources and follows directly from the mathematical definition of the operation.]

Synthetic waveform generation produces acceleration time histories from analytical or stochastic models of the source, propagation medium, and site response, without reference to any specific measured record. Stochastic methods parameterize the source spectral shape using source models, apply distance-dependent path filters, and optionally apply a site transfer function, then generate a time series by sampling a random phase spectrum or by superimposing point-source contributions from a finite-fault model. [ARXIV:2309.03447] The resulting waveform is a model realization whose statistical properties are governed by the chosen parameterization rather than by the physics of any single measured event. For blast vibration sources, stochastic synthesis would require parameterization of the explosive source spectrum, the near-field transmission characteristics of the rock mass, and the site amplification at the analysis boundary -- parameters that require calibration from measured records and introduce model-form uncertainty at each stage. Amplitude-scaled measured records, by contrast, embed the actual propagation physics of the measurement without imposing any model structure on the source or path; all waveform complexity is inherited from the real event, and the only introduced parameter is the scalar scale factor. [WEB:https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-USE-OF-REAL-EARTHQUAKE-ACCELEROGRAMS-AS-INPUT-Bommer-Acevedo/84f53edfeb3a19a7e9601645ac5bfc09795ec228]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The characterization of stochastic waveform synthesis and its parameter requirements is supported by ARXIV:2309.03447 (earthquake waveform synthesis) and by general stochastic simulation methodology. However, the specific application to blast source stochastic synthesis is less directly cited in a peer-reviewed source in the search results; the reasoning is an engineering extrapolation from earthquake stochastic synthesis principles to the blast domain, which is physically reasonable but warrants the MEDIUM confidence rating. Bommer and Acevedo (2004) supports the claim about the advantage of real records in embedding measured propagation physics.]

Calibrated blast source modelling develops a site-specific deterministic or semi-empirical model of the explosive source and its near-field propagation, calibrated against a collection of measured blast waveforms. Such a model predicts waveform characteristics at new locations or for new charge configurations by applying the calibrated source-path-site transfer functions to a defined scenario. The predicted waveform is a physics-constrained output of the calibrated model, conditioned on the model parameters and the scenario inputs. This approach differs from amplitude scaling in that the waveform is generated by the model rather than derived from a measured record, and in that the model must be validated against independent measurements before its outputs can be relied upon for engineering design. [WEB:https://www.dynonobel.com/products-services/products/software/nobel-fire/] For a TSF dynamic response review whose scope is limited to constructing scenario demand histories at specified amplitude levels from an existing measured record archive, a calibrated blast source model would add substantial development and validation effort without necessarily improving the conservatism or defensibility of the resulting demand histories relative to amplitude-scaled measured records. The calibrated model approach is appropriate when the goal is to extrapolate blast vibration predictions to unmeasured locations or charge configurations; amplitude scaling is appropriate when the goal is to adjust the amplitude of a measured event to a scenario level while preserving all other waveform characteristics. [KB:T3.compile.research.md]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The description of calibrated blast source modelling and its distinction from amplitude scaling is engineering-reasonable and consistent with general understanding of predictive blast vibration modelling. The Dyno Nobel software reference is a commercial product page rather than a peer-reviewed source and is used here only as an illustrative reference for the category of calibrated source model; the broader methodological argument does not depend on this single commercial source. KB:T3 supports the general principle of using processed measured records as engineering inputs rather than modeled representations. The MEDIUM rating reflects the limited peer-reviewed citation coverage for the blast-specific calibrated source modelling argument.]

## SLOT 5: Defensible Wording for Scenario Demand Histories

The acceleration time histories applied in this analysis are amplitude-scaled versions of blast vibration records measured during production blasting at the project site. Each component of the measured three-component acceleration record was multiplied by a scale factor defined as the ratio of the target scenario peak acceleration for that component to the measured peak acceleration of the original record, producing scaled acceleration histories at the defined scenario amplitude level. The resulting time series preserve the frequency content, duration, phase structure, pulse shape, and non-stationary character of the measured event; only the overall amplitude level has been modified. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf][ARXIV:2305.05631]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph describes the amplitude scaling procedure accurately and is fully consistent with the preserved properties established in Slot 2 and supported by NIST GCR 11-917-15 and ARXIV:2305.05631. The wording is based on the established definition of amplitude scaling in the cited literature. No factual claims beyond the definition of the operation are made.]

These records are described as scenario demand histories in the following sense: the waveform shape, frequency content, and duration are those of the measured blast event, and the peak amplitude has been adjusted to the scenario level by multiplication with the defined scale factor; no assertion is made that a blast event producing exactly these waveforms at the scenario amplitude was measured at the analysis boundary. The source-path-site conditions embedded in the measured records are treated as representative of the scenario loading environment on the basis that the measurement location is proximate to the analysis domain and the blast parameters of the measurement campaign are consistent with the scenario design. [WEB:https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-USE-OF-REAL-EARTHQUAKE-ACCELEROGRAMS-AS-INPUT-Bommer-Acevedo/84f53edfeb3a19a7e9601645ac5bfc09795ec228] This representativeness assumption is a declaration, not a derived result, and its validity depends on the engineering judgment of the analyst regarding the spatial and parametric consistency of the source-path-site conditions between the measurement and analysis locations.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between "scenario demand history derived by amplitude scaling" and "measured event at the scenario amplitude" is well established in the Bommer and Acevedo (2004) framework for the use of real records. The statement that representativeness is a declaration rather than a derived result is consistent with the scenario consistency requirement discussed in NIST GCR 11-917-15 and the bias discussion in Luco and Bazzurro (2007). The wording preserves intellectual honesty about the nature of the assumption without undermining the defensibility of the method.]

The horizontal and vertical components of the scaled records were independently scaled to the defined scenario peak acceleration for each component. This procedure maximizes each component at the scenario amplitude level and produces a three-component input whose inter-component amplitude ratios differ from those of the original measured event; the combined input constitutes a conservative amplitude envelope rather than a simulation of a single physical blast geometry. Response quantities that are sensitive to the simultaneous spatial combination of components -- including rotational displacements, directional deformation patterns, and the spatial distribution of shear strain within the tailings mass -- are therefore interpreted as conservative envelope estimates conditional on the independent peak-scaling convention. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.695][WEB:https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0186.pdf]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of independently scaled multi-component inputs as conservative amplitude envelopes rather than physical event simulations is supported by Luco and Bazzurro (2007) and the multi-component scaling discussion in the cited ground motion selection references. The statement that response quantities sensitive to component combination are conditional on the scaling convention is a necessary logical consequence of the limitations established in Slot 3. No source contradiction detected.]

The scaled acceleration histories define the kinematic boundary conditions applied to the numerical model of the tailings storage facility; they do not, by themselves, constitute a prediction of pore pressure development in the saturated tailings. Pore pressure generation under cyclic blast loading is governed by the applied cyclic stress ratio, the number and amplitude of loading cycles, the initial effective stress state, the relative density of the tailings, and the undrained cyclic behavior of the material as represented by the constitutive model. The acceleration input provides the dynamic loading; the material response is a model output.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The multi-parameter dependence of pore pressure generation in saturated tailings is supported by the tailings seismic paper and KB:T3, and is consistent with standard liquefaction analysis methodology. The constraint in the task file requiring that no direct pore-pressure prediction from records alone be claimed is satisfied by this wording. The characterization of the acceleration histories as kinematic boundary conditions whose pore pressure consequences depend on the constitutive model is technically accurate and standard in nonlinear effective-stress dynamic analysis.]
