### SLOT 1: Authoritative EMD/EEMD Filtering References

The most authoritative starting point is Huang et al. (1998), because it introduced empirical mode decomposition as the decomposition stage of the Hilbert-Huang transform for nonlinear and non-stationary records and established the intrinsic mode function and residual reconstruction framework used by later filtering studies. This source supports EMD as an adaptive decomposition of a measured record, but it does not by itself justify treating any retained intrinsic mode function as a uniquely physical engineering component. [DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193] The central EEMD reference is Wu and Huang (2009), because it defines ensemble empirical mode decomposition as a noise-assisted method in which finite-amplitude white noise is added over repeated trials and ensemble averaging is used to reduce mode mixing and obtain a scale-organized decomposition. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047] Flandrin, Rilling, and Goncalves (2004) and Wu and Huang (2004) are also authoritative for filtering claims because they connect EMD behavior to filter-bank-like and noise-scale behavior, which is the main basis for describing IMFs as adaptive scale bands rather than preassigned physical modes. [DOI:10.1109/lsp.2003.821662][DOI:10.1098/rspa.2003.1221]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The paragraph is supported by DOI-verified foundational EMD, EEMD, filter-bank, and white-noise characterization sources, and it avoids unsupported quantitative claims or physical overinterpretation.]

For engineering filtering rather than basic decomposition, Boudraa and Cexus (2007) and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) are more directly relevant because they treat EMD as a signal filtering or denoising tool and address component selection or thresholding in measurement and signal-processing contexts. [DOI:10.1109/tim.2007.907967][DOI:10.1109/tsp.2009.2013885] Huang and Wu (2008) is a useful review source because it summarizes Hilbert-Huang transform applications in geophysical records and discusses open problems, making it appropriate for limitation language in an engineering report. [DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228] For transient acceleration histories, the EMD/EEMD sources are best paired with strong-motion processing references such as Boore and Bommer (2005), Boore (2005), and Boore and Akkar (2003), because those papers document the consequences of filtering, baseline correction, and filter choices on accelerograms and response spectra. [DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160][DOI:10.1002/eqe.299]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The cited sources directly identify EMD filtering, EMD denoising, HHT review context, and strong-motion processing consequences; the use of seismic strong-motion literature as transient-acceleration support is a methodological analogy rather than an uncited blast-specific standard.]

### SLOT 2: Supported Component Removal Practices

The literature supports component removal only as a documented filtering or detrending operation followed by reconstruction, not as automatic evidence that the removed or retained IMFs are physically unique blast-wave components. EMD/EEMD decompositions are adaptive and reconstructive, so a processed history can be formed by summing selected IMFs and, when appropriate, a residual term; however, the selection criterion has to be stated as an engineering or signal-processing rule, such as removal of components dominated by high-frequency noise, removal of a low-frequency trend, or retention of components inside a target response band. [DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047][DOI:10.1109/lsp.2003.821662] A defensible report therefore presents IMF removal as scale-selective filtering based on evidence from the record, not as a decomposition into uniquely named source mechanisms. [DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The citations support adaptive decomposition, EEMD reconstruction context, filter-bank-like behavior, and review-level limitations; the paragraph states a conservative methodological interpretation consistent with those sources.]

Removal of early, high-frequency IMFs is supported when those IMFs are shown to be dominated by measurement noise or by frequencies outside the engineering response range of interest. Boudraa and Cexus support EMD-based signal filtering, and Kopsinis and McLaughlin support denoising approaches that treat IMF content with thresholding logic rather than a blanket rule that the first IMF is always noise. [DOI:10.1109/tim.2007.907967][DOI:10.1109/tsp.2009.2013885] Removal of a residual or trend term can be defensible when the residual represents baseline drift or nonphysical low-frequency contamination, but the same operation can also remove real long-period motion; in acceleration-history applications it therefore has to be treated as a high-pass or trend-removal choice whose consequences are checked in integrated velocity, displacement, and response spectra. [DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160][DOI:10.1002/eqe.299]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The EMD filtering and denoising sources support high-frequency component filtering, while the strong-motion sources support concern about baseline, drift, and spectral consequences of filtering.]

Selection of nonadjacent or middle IMFs is also supported only when the selection is tied to a stated diagnostic, such as frequency content, energy contribution, correlation with the target signal, response-spectral relevance, or comparison with independent instrumentation or physics. Rilling and Flandrin show that EMD separation of nearby frequency content depends on signal conditions, so component selection cannot be assumed to isolate every true modal contribution in a short transient record. [DOI:10.1109/tsp.2007.906771] EEMD reduces mode mixing by adding noise-assisted ensemble averaging, but the method still depends on ensemble size, added-noise amplitude, sifting choices, and endpoint treatment, so retained IMF groups are better described as selected scale bands than as verified physical modes. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The cited EMD separation and EEMD review sources directly support the limitation on interpreting selected IMFs as physically unique components.]

### SLOT 3: Known Limitations To Report

Mode mixing is a primary limitation to report. EEMD was proposed specifically to reduce the tendency of EMD to place disparate scales in the same IMF or split a similar scale across several IMFs, but its remedy uses added white noise and ensemble averaging rather than a deterministic physical separation. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047] This means that the reported processing has to identify the added-noise level, ensemble size, sifting or stopping criterion, randomization policy when relevant, and any post-processing or alignment method used to compare IMFs across trials. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The EEMD source supports the noise-assisted ensemble mechanism and mode-mixing purpose, and the review source supports reporting methodological dependencies and open issues.]

End effects, non-uniqueness, and parameter sensitivity are also material limitations. EMD envelopes are built from local extrema, so behavior near the beginning and end of a short record can be sensitive to interpolation and boundary extension choices; this is especially important for transient vibration histories in which the main pulse, decay tail, or baseline segment may occur near a boundary. [DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228] The decomposition is not a fixed orthogonal transform with a predeclared basis, and different sifting, stopping, noise, and endpoint choices can change the IMF set; therefore, the same measured history can yield different but plausible component partitions. [DOI:10.1109/lsp.2003.821662][DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The cited EMD and HHT sources support adaptive, algorithm-dependent decomposition and open limitations; the added emphasis on short blast-like transients is a reasonable engineering extrapolation but is not directly validated by a blast-specific source in the paragraph.]

Loss of physical interpretability is a limitation whenever IMFs are used for report-level engineering claims. EMD and EEMD components are derived from local time-scale behavior, so the existence of an IMF group does not by itself demonstrate a separate blast source, wave path, soil layer response, sensor artifact, or structural mode. [DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1109/tsp.2007.906771] Filtering can also alter peak values, apparent pulse duration, low-frequency drift, and response demands; those consequences are well documented in strong-motion filtering and baseline processing literature and are relevant when processed acceleration histories are used downstream. [DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1002/eqe.299]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The cited EMD separation source supports caution about physical interpretation, and the strong-motion processing sources support concern about filtering effects on time histories and response spectra.]

### SLOT 4: Validation Checks For EEMD-Filtered Histories

Validation of an EEMD-filtered acceleration history is strongest when it compares the raw record, the retained reconstruction, and the removed residual content in both time and frequency domains. Peak preservation checks include the signed and absolute peak acceleration, peak timing, pulse polarity, and local pulse shape near the peak, because a filter that improves visual smoothness can still alter the controlling demand in a transient history. [DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160] Baseline and drift checks include numerical integration to velocity and displacement, comparison of terminal velocity and displacement trends, and review of any low-frequency residual removed from the reconstruction. [DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The strong-motion processing citations directly support the importance of filtering, baseline correction, and integrated-history consequences for acceleration records.]

Energy and frequency-content checks are defensible because EMD filtering is scale-selective. The report can compare root-mean-square acceleration, summed squared acceleration, cumulative signal energy, Fourier or power spectral density estimates, Hilbert marginal spectra when used, and the frequency content of removed IMFs to confirm that the operation removed the intended scale range rather than suppressing engineering demand. [DOI:10.1109/tim.2007.907967][DOI:10.1109/tsp.2009.2013885][DOI:10.1098/rspa.2003.1221] Response-spectrum checks are important when the processed acceleration is used as an engineering input, because response spectra can be sensitive to filtering choices and the difference may be consequential even when the acceleration trace appears acceptable. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.299][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The paragraph combines EMD filtering and denoising sources with response-spectrum filtering sources, and the proposed diagnostics are consistent with the cited methods without adding unsupported numerical thresholds.]

Repeatability and visual diagnostics are also necessary for EEMD-filtered histories. Because EEMD depends on added-noise amplitude and ensemble averaging, the filtered result can be checked by rerunning the decomposition with the same documented parameters and, where relevant, with alternative random seeds or nearby ensemble settings to confirm that retained scale bands and validation metrics are stable. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228] Visual review includes overlaid raw and filtered acceleration traces, removed-component traces, endpoint behavior, pre-event or post-event baseline segments when available, and plots of cumulative residuals or integrated histories so that the report shows whether the reconstruction preserves the features that control engineering response. [DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The EEMD citations support ensemble-parameter sensitivity, and the strong-motion citations support visual, baseline, and integrated-history review as validation of processed acceleration data.]

### SLOT 5: Description Without Overclaiming

EEMD filtering is best described as an adaptive, data-driven filtering and reconstruction procedure applied to a measured vibration history. A conservative description states that selected IMFs were retained or removed according to documented engineering criteria, and that the resulting reconstruction was accepted only after checks on peak response, energy, frequency content, baseline behavior, and response spectra. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047][DOI:10.1109/tim.2007.907967][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007] The description avoids stating that retained modes are physically unique blast-wave components unless separate evidence from instrumentation, site physics, wave-propagation modeling, or independent measurements supports that interpretation. [DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1109/tsp.2007.906771]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The EEMD and EMD filtering citations support adaptive reconstruction language, while the EMD separation citation supports the caution against assigning unique physical meaning to retained IMFs.]

Appropriate terminology includes "retained IMF group," "removed scale band," "EEMD-filtered reconstruction," "adaptive denoising," and "trend or residual removal," because those terms describe the processing operation without asserting a source mechanism. [DOI:10.1109/lsp.2003.821662][DOI:10.1109/tsp.2009.2013885] Terms such as "true blast component," "physical mode," or "isolated wave type" require additional support and are not justified by the decomposition alone, because EMD separation depends on signal conditions and EEMD depends on noise-assisted ensemble choices. [DOI:10.1109/tsp.2007.906771][DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The citations support scale-band and denoising terminology while also supporting limits on physical interpretation and dependency on decomposition conditions.]

A publication-ready limitation statement can say that EEMD was used to reduce unwanted scale content while preserving defined engineering measures, and that the filtered record is a processed representation of the measured history rather than a recovered unique physical waveform. [DOI:10.1109/tim.2007.907967][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007] The report can further state that conclusions based on the filtered history are conditional on the documented EEMD parameters, component-selection rule, and validation results, and that raw and filtered histories remain distinguishable in the record archive and subsequent calculations. [DOI:10.1142/s1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007rg000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The cited filtering, EEMD, HHT review, and acceleration-processing sources support the constrained claim language and the requirement to tie conclusions to documented processing and validation.]
