### SLOT 1: Authoritative Guidance Documents Covering Blasting Effects on TSFs

The international industry standards that most directly address dynamic loading, vibration monitoring, pore-pressure response, and liquefaction screening for tailings storage facilities are the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM, 2020) and the IFC Environmental, Health, and Safety Guidelines for Mining (2007). The GISTM, developed under the auspices of the United Nations Environment Programme, the International Council on Mining and Metals, and the Principles for Responsible Investment, requires operators to establish an interdisciplinary knowledge base encompassing geotechnical and seismicity information, to document design criteria explicitly, and to implement integrated performance monitoring throughout the facility life cycle. [KB:T1.compile.research.md] The standard does not prescribe fixed numerical vibration thresholds for blast-induced loading on TSFs; it frames safety as a risk-management obligation demanding project-specific assessment and continuous monitoring as the means of demonstrating performance. The IFC EHS Guidelines for Mining (2007) require that tailings impoundments and dams be planned, designed, and operated such that geotechnical risks are assessed and managed throughout the mine life cycle, and they require designs to reflect internationally recognised engineering practices and local seismic conditions, treating geotechnical risk assessment - including blast-induced ground motion effects on tailings facilities - as a distinct discipline rather than an extension of building-vibration compliance. ### SLOT 3: Quantitative Acceptance Criteria versus Project-Specific Assessment

None of the primary authoritative sources for TSF safety - GISTM, ANCOLD (2019), ICOLD bulletins 98, 104, 121, 148, and 194, or the IFC EHS Guidelines for Mining - prescribes a universal fixed blast-vibration threshold expressed as a limiting peak particle velocity for protecting a tailings storage facility. The absence of a universal vibration limit for TSFs reflects the established recognition in geotechnical dam engineering that the dynamic response of a saturated tailings facility depends on material properties, fabric, degree of saturation, drainage conditions, confining stress, pore-pressure history, the geometry of the embankment, and the proximity and characteristics of the blasting source. None of these site-specific factors are captured by a generic PPV criterion derived from research on vibration effects in rigid or masonry building structures. ### SLOT 4: Report Framing to Distinguish Criteria, Monitoring Requirements, and Assumptions

A professional technical report on the effects of nearby blasting on a TSF must clearly delineate four distinct categories of statement: published reference criteria drawn from identified standards; project-specific criteria established for the facility in question by the Engineer of Record; monitoring requirements arising from regulatory obligations, best-practice standards, and design documentation; and analysis assumptions made in the assessment. Conflating these categories - in particular, applying a criterion developed for a different receiver type, such as a building-damage PPV threshold from a vibration standard, as though it governs TSF structural performance - misrepresents the state of professional practice and may produce technically indefensible conclusions that expose the report to challenge during regulatory review or independent assessment. ### SLOT 5: Source-Backed Terminology for TSF Response to Blasting

The term "dynamic loading" appears in the ICOLD bulletins on tailings dams and in the ANCOLD guidelines with a consistent meaning: any time-varying force or acceleration applied to the structure, its embankment, or its foundation, encompassing earthquake-induced ground motion, blast-induced ground vibration, and in some formulations operational loads from heavy equipment. [WEB:https://ancold.org.au/product/guidelines-on-tailings-dams-planning-design-construction-operation-and-closure-revision-1-july-2019/] [WEB:https://klohn.com/wp-content/uploads/B194-ICOLD-Tailings-Dam-Safety.pdf] In the tailings dam literature, dynamic loading is treated as a stability-relevant load case that must be considered alongside static loading in the design and performance assessment of the facility and that may govern design where the tailings or foundation material is susceptible to strength loss under cyclic excitation. The term does not carry an implication about the source of the loading; it is applicable to both seismic and blast events provided the dynamic nature of the loading is established.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The use of "dynamic loading" in the context of ANCOLD and ICOLD tailings dam guidance is consistent with the content of those documents as confirmed by web search results. However, the specific application to both seismic and blast events in the same ANCOLD or ICOLD text is not directly quoted in the retrieved search results, which describe seismic loading primarily. The characterisation is a reasonable and professionally defensible extrapolation of the term's standard technical meaning in geotechnical engineering.]

The term "transient vibration" is used in the vibration measurement literature, including ISO 4866:2010, to describe a vibration event of finite duration that does not persist as continuous or steady-state excitation. [KB:T1.compile.research.md] Blast-induced ground motion constitutes a transient vibration event: it consists of a short-duration stress-wave sequence generated by detonation, characterised by a rapidly rising wavefront, a frequency spectrum determined by the charge weight, delay pattern, and geological propagation path, and a decay envelope that is a function of the scaled distance to the receptor. The term is appropriate for characterising the nature of the blast-induced ground motion as a physical input to the TSF assessment but carries no connotations about the structural response or the stability outcome; those are assessed through the geotechnical analysis.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterisation of ISO 4866:2010 and the term "transient vibration" is drawn directly from the KB (T1.compile.research.md), which is treated as authoritative. "Pore-pressure generation" and "excess pore pressure" are the standard geotechnical terms for the increase in pore-water pressure above the in-situ hydrostatic equilibrium produced by undrained cyclic or transient loading. "Cyclic mobility" and "flow liquefaction" are the two principal distinct failure modes associated with excess pore-pressure generation in saturated soils under cyclic loading, and their distinction is essential for accurate TSF reporting. Flow liquefaction occurs in contractive soils - including loose, saturated, fine-grained tailings - where the residual undrained shear strength mobilised after triggering is insufficient to maintain stability under the existing static shear stresses, resulting in a catastrophic, sustained loss of resistance and runout. Cyclic mobility occurs in soils that retain a positive residual undrained shear strength after triggering, producing transient loss of effective stress and large cyclic strains without a sustained flow failure. [WEB:https://itascasoftware.com/resources/when-cyclic-mobility-isnt-flow-liquefaction/] ICOLD Bulletin 98 and the Idriss and Boulanger (2008) framework distinguish these mechanisms, and ANCOLD (2019) recommends conservatively assuming flow liquefaction for saturated, brittle, contractive tailings absent evidence supporting a less severe classification. [WEB:https://ancold.org.au/product/guidelines-on-tailings-dams-planning-design-construction-operation-and-closure-revision-1-july-2019/] In a technical report, the failure mode being assessed should be named explicitly, and the material evidence - penetration resistance profile, gradation, plasticity index - that supports the classification should be cited.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between flow liquefaction and cyclic mobility is a well-established geotechnical concept confirmed by the web search results, including the Itasca resource and the general liquefaction literature. "Liquefaction potential" is used in the Youd et al. $$FS_L = \frac{CRR_{7.5} \cdot MSF \cdot K_\sigma}{CSR}$$

where $CRR_{7.5}$ is the cyclic resistance ratio corrected to a reference earthquake magnitude of $M_w = 7.5$, $MSF$ is the magnitude scaling factor that adjusts for the duration and equivalent cycles of the actual event, and $K_\sigma$ is the overburden correction factor. When a blast event rather than an earthquake provides the dynamic demand, the CSR must be estimated from the blast-induced ground motion parameters and a representative equivalent number of uniform stress cycles must be assigned to the transient loading, making $FS_L$ a conditional quantity whose value is governed by these project-specific input choices. "Deformation" and "stability" are used in the ANCOLD guidelines and ICOLD bulletins as distinct but related performance parameters for tailings dam assessment under dynamic loading. Stability refers to the resistance of the TSF embankment and its foundation to slope failure, evaluated as a factor of safety against global instability; ANCOLD (2019) prescribes a minimum value of 1.1 under transient seismic conditions as a quantitative performance target. [WEB:https://ancold.org.au/product/guidelines-on-tailings-dams-planning-design-construction-operation-and-closure-revision-1-july-2019/] Deformation refers to the permanent displacements accumulated during a dynamic event, evaluated through analyses such as the Newmark sliding-block method or fully coupled dynamic numerical analysis; it is possible for a structure to remain nominally stable - maintaining a factor of safety above unity throughout the event - while accumulating permanent deformations that damage the crest, impair drainage elements, or compromise the freeboard required for safe operation. A technical report should distinguish stability assessment from deformation assessment and should state explicitly which analyses have been performed, which have been omitted, and why.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between deformation and stability as performance parameters in dam engineering is a standard professional convention supported by the ANCOLD guidelines (confirmed via web search) and consistent with ICOLD practice.
