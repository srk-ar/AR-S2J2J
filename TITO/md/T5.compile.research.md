### SLOT 1: References Connecting Blast Loading to Saturated-Tailings Response

The project-relevant blast literature first establishes the measured excitation, not the saturated-tailings response. The KB-supported standards and regulatory sources identify blast vibration as a short-duration transient ground-motion record that can be described by source timing, charge window, distance, peak particle velocity, airblast, and seismographic record details; the same KB material also distinguishes building damage, human response, monitoring, and TSF geotechnical performance as separate reporting categories [@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref007][@Ref008][@Ref001]. This is important for the TSF question because blast records can define a dynamic demand history, but the cited blast-vibration standards do not provide a calibrated excess pore-pressure or liquefaction triggering equation for saturated tailings [@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref001].

The technical bridge from blast loading to saturated-tailings response therefore comes from soil dynamics and liquefaction mechanics rather than from ordinary blast-damage criteria. The NCEER liquefaction workshop summary, Seed-type cyclic-stress procedures, and effective-stress pore-pressure models connect undrained cyclic shear stress, cyclic resistance, number of equivalent cycles, and pore-pressure buildup to liquefaction or cyclic mobility in saturated sands and silty sands [@Ref002][@Ref003]. Bachrach, Nur, and Agnon add a complementary dynamic-poroelastic reference for soft saturated sediments by showing that cyclic stress in a fluid-saturated porous layer can raise pore pressure through coupled solid-fluid wave response; Biot's low- and high-frequency poroelastic wave formulations are the underlying theoretical family for that type of short-duration coupled response [@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref006].

Recent computational and data-driven liquefaction papers are useful as benchmarks for irregular or short-pulse demand because they treat the loading history itself as an input rather than reducing it only to a single peak value. Kuhn, Renken, Mixsell, and Kramer used undrained cyclic shearing simulations with nonuniform amplitudes and irregular seismic sequences and reported that stress-based measures were efficient predictors of initial liquefaction and pore-pressure rise [@Ref007]. Choi and Kumar similarly emphasized that shear-stress history, relative density, prior loading amplitude, and density effects control pore-pressure response in cyclic simple shear tests, including a shielding effect when later cycles are below earlier peak amplitudes [@Ref008].

For mine-tailings and TSF context, the stronger references concern material state, liquefied strength, runout, and governance rather than a universal blast-triggering equation. The KB cites the IFC mining guidance and Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management as requiring TSF geotechnical knowledge, seismicity context, design criteria, independent review, and monitoring, while recent tailings-failure simulations show that liquefied-tailings properties and strain-softening assumptions can control runout predictions after seismic liquefaction [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref001][@Ref009]. Sottile, Cueto, and Sfriso also illustrate that tailings liquefaction assessment depends on material stiffness, undrained softening, residual strength, and work input rather than on motion records alone, although their procedure addresses static triggering rather than blast-induced cyclic pore-pressure prediction [@Ref010].

### SLOT 2: Model Families and Equations

The first model family is the simplified cyclic-stress framework, in which earthquake or vibration demand is represented by a cyclic stress ratio and compared with a cyclic resistance ratio obtained from penetration resistance, shear-wave velocity, or laboratory cyclic tests. In its common level-ground earthquake form, the demand index is expressed as follows [@Ref002][@Ref011][@Ref012][@Ref013].

$$
CSR = \frac{\tau_{cyc}}{\sigma'_{v0}} \approx 0.65 \frac{a_{max}}{g}\frac{\sigma_{v0}}{\sigma'_{v0}} r_d
$$

In this expression, $CSR$ is cyclic stress ratio, $\tau_{cyc}$ is representative cyclic shear stress, $\sigma'_{v0}$ is initial vertical effective stress, $a_{max}$ is peak horizontal acceleration, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, $\sigma_{v0}$ is total vertical stress, and $r_d$ is the depth-reduction factor. The same family usually expresses the triggering check through a factor of safety that applies duration or magnitude scaling and static-shear or overburden corrections to the resistance term [@Ref002][@Ref014].

$$
FS_{liq} = \frac{CRR_{M=7.5} MSF K_\sigma K_\alpha}{CSR}
$$

The second model family is pore-pressure generation or effective-stress modeling, where the state variable of interest is commonly the excess pore-pressure ratio. Initial liquefaction in saturated cohesionless soil is often associated with $r_u$ approaching 1.0, although cyclic mobility and flow failure distinctions require interpretation of strain, stress reversal, density, and static shear stress rather than a pore-pressure ratio alone [@Ref002][@Ref003].

$$
r_u = \frac{\Delta u}{\sigma'_{v0}}
$$

In this expression, $r_u$ is the excess pore-pressure ratio and $\Delta u$ is generated excess pore pressure. Empirical pore-pressure models, cyclic shear-volume coupling models, and effective-stress constitutive models relate incremental pore-pressure generation to cyclic shear strain or stress amplitude, volumetric contraction tendency under undrained loading, relative density or state parameter, confining stress, cyclic history, drainage boundary conditions, and permeability [@Ref003][@Ref008].

The third family is advanced nonlinear effective-stress or critical-state constitutive modeling for saturated porous media. These models solve coupled stress-deformation and pore-fluid response and require calibration of stiffness, damping or hysteresis, dilatancy, contraction, critical-state line or state parameter, permeability, compressibility, and liquefied or residual strength; they are better suited than a peak-motion index when the objective is time-dependent pore-pressure response in tailings [@Ref004][@Ref009][@Ref010]. Discrete-element and data-driven models provide additional research benchmarks for irregular cyclic histories, but they still require material calibration and should not be treated as a direct conversion from PPV, PGA, or frequency to pore-pressure ratio [@Ref007][@Ref008].

### SLOT 3: Frequency, Duration, and Short High-Frequency Pulses

The earthquake-derived cyclic-stress procedures treat duration mainly through magnitude scaling factors or equivalent-cycle concepts, not through a direct high-frequency blast-pulse rule. This means that a short blast record cannot be interpreted only by its peak particle velocity or peak acceleration if the engineering question is pore-pressure generation; the cyclic stress amplitude, number of damaging cycles, stress reversal, drainage condition, and material state must also be considered [@Ref002][@Ref014]. For blast-like loading, this creates an explicit limitation: earthquake liquefaction procedures can provide a conservative screening analogy, but they are not a calibrated transformation from a few milliseconds of high-frequency vibration to excess pore-pressure ratio in tailings [@Ref001][@Ref002].

Short, high-frequency pulses may be mechanically important if they generate cyclic shear stresses in susceptible saturated material under effectively undrained conditions, but their pore-pressure significance depends on coupling and filtering through the ground and the TSF. Dynamic poroelasticity explicitly includes frequency-dependent solid-fluid interaction, permeability, fluid compressibility, and wave-propagation effects, so it is the theoretical family most directly relevant to short-duration saturated-porous-media response [@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref004]. However, that theory does not remove the need for site-specific material properties and boundary conditions, and it should not be used as a generic blast-liquefaction criterion without calibration [@Ref004].

Irregular-history studies also caution against reducing demand to a single maximum value. Kuhn and coauthors found that scalar measures of irregular cyclic histories can be ranked for severity and that stress-based measures were more efficient than some alternatives for predicting initial liquefaction and pore-pressure rise, while Choi and Kumar showed that prior stress history and relative density can change the pore-pressure response to later cycles [@Ref007][@Ref008]. For blast records, the implication is that the damaging content of a motion history depends on the effective cyclic shear-stress sequence transmitted into the tailings, not simply on the highest-frequency oscillation or the largest recorded peak [@Ref007][@Ref008].

The practical duration limit is therefore conservative and methodological. A few high-frequency cycles may have limited pore-pressure consequence if their stress amplitudes are below prior damaging amplitudes, if the generated strains are small, or if drainage and material stiffness prevent significant effective-stress loss, but a short duration is not by itself proof of safety in loose, contractive, saturated fine tailings [@Ref008][@Ref002]. Conversely, a high PPV or high PGA record is not proof of liquefaction unless the record is converted through an appropriate stress-transfer model and checked against calibrated cyclic resistance or effective-stress response [@Ref002][@Ref011].

### SLOT 4: Material Parameters and Motion-Record Limits

Evaluation of pore-pressure generation or cyclic resistance in saturated tailings requires material, state, and boundary-condition parameters that are not present in an acceleration or velocity trace. The required parameters commonly include stratigraphy, saturation and phreatic surface, total and effective stresses, unit weights, fines content and plasticity, grain-size distribution, void ratio or relative density, fabric or depositional history, overconsolidation or aging, static shear stress ratio, initial shear stress bias, hydraulic conductivity, compressibility, small-strain shear modulus, modulus reduction and damping behavior, cyclic resistance from laboratory or in situ correlations, state parameter or critical-state line, and undrained peak and residual or liquefied strength [@Ref002][@Ref011][@Ref012][@Ref013][@Ref010][@Ref009].

Acceleration and velocity records can provide demand-side descriptors such as PGA, PGV, Arias-type energy measures if computed, dominant frequency, duration, pulse count, and record timing, but they do not directly identify density, contractiveness, permeability, drainage path length, pore-fluid compressibility, cyclic resistance, state parameter, or residual strength. They also do not by themselves provide the shear-stress history inside a tailings layer, because that requires a site-response or stress-transfer model with the relevant geometry, impedance, stiffness, damping, and boundary conditions [@Ref002][@Ref004].

Motion processing adds another limit. The KB-supported EMD/EEMD and strong-motion processing references state that filtering, baseline correction, component removal, endpoint treatment, and reconstruction choices can alter peak values, pulse duration, low-frequency drift, integrated velocity and displacement, response spectra, and engineering demand measures [@Ref008][@Ref009][@Ref010][@Ref015]. Therefore, a processed blast or scenario record is a documented representation of the measured history, not a direct measurement of pore pressure, effective stress, cyclic strain, or liquefaction state in the tailings [@Ref002][@Ref007][@Ref015].

The parameters that cannot be inferred from motion records alone are the controlling parameters for a calibrated pore-pressure prediction. These include the cyclic pore-pressure generation law, cyclic resistance ratio, liquefaction triggering strain criterion, drainage condition during the event, permeability and drainage boundaries, initial effective stress state, contractive or dilative tendency, critical-state parameters, residual or liquefied strength, and pore-pressure dissipation rate [@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref008][@Ref010]. In the absence of pore-pressure instrumentation or calibrated effective-stress modeling, the records support demand screening and comparison, not a quantitative statement that $r_u$ reached a specified value [@Ref002][@Ref015].

### SLOT 5: Conservative Report Wording

The measured and scenario blast-vibration histories are interpreted as dynamic demand indicators for the TSF, not as a calibrated pore-pressure generation or liquefaction prediction model. The records describe the timing, amplitude, frequency content, and duration of ground-motion demand at the measurement location or selected scenario point, while saturated-tailings response depends on the in situ effective stress state, material state, drainage conditions, cyclic resistance, stress-transfer path, and pore-pressure generation characteristics [@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref003].

The analysis therefore should not state that a recorded PPV, PGA, dominant frequency, or filtered acceleration history directly proves or excludes liquefaction of the tailings. Such records may be used to rank events, identify controlling scenarios, compare pulse duration and amplitude, and select representative demand histories for further geotechnical analysis, but a quantitative estimate of excess pore-pressure ratio, cyclic mobility, or liquefaction triggering would require material-specific calibration and an effective-stress or cyclic-resistance model [@Ref002][@Ref004][@Ref008].

Where earthquake-derived liquefaction concepts are used, the report should describe them as screening analogies for cyclic demand and not as blast-specific acceptance criteria. The duration and equivalent-cycle assumptions embedded in earthquake liquefaction procedures are not automatically transferable to a short high-frequency blast pulse, and any conversion from recorded motion to cyclic stress in tailings should state the assumed site-response model, stress-transfer method, drainage condition, material parameters, and uncertainty [@Ref002][@Ref014][@Ref007].

Building-damage, human-response, and general blast-monitoring criteria should remain separate from the TSF pore-pressure question. The KB-supported standards and regulatory references can support how blast records are documented and how charge, distance, and vibration measures are reported, but they should not be presented as liquefaction thresholds for saturated fine tailings unless project-specific geotechnical analysis establishes that relationship [@Ref003][@Ref004][@Ref005][@Ref007][@Ref008][@Ref001].

Conservative final wording can state that the available blast records provide a defensible basis for dynamic-demand characterization, scenario selection, and comparison of measured and processed histories. It can also state that evaluation of excess pore-pressure generation, cyclic mobility, or liquefaction in saturated tailings remains conditional on independent geotechnical characterization, pore-pressure or cyclic laboratory data, and calibrated effective-stress or cyclic-resistance analysis, so conclusions from the present record review should be limited to demand indicators unless those additional inputs are introduced [@Ref015][@Ref003][@Ref010][@Ref009].
