# EEMD Filtering and Reconstruction: Claim Support, Limitations, and Validation

### SLOT 1: Authoritative EMD/EEMD Filtering References

The most authoritative starting point for EMD-based filtering claims in engineering vibration applications is Huang et al. (1998), which introduced empirical mode decomposition as the decomposition stage of the Hilbert-Huang transform for nonlinear and non-stationary records and established the intrinsic mode function and residual reconstruction framework used by all later filtering studies. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref001][DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193] That source supports EMD as an adaptive decomposition of a measured record, but it does not by itself justify treating any retained intrinsic mode function as a uniquely physical engineering component.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of Huang et al. (1998) as the foundational reference for EMD, and the qualification that it does not establish physical uniqueness of IMFs, is accurately stated and consistent with [KB:T3.compile.research.md]; DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193 confirms the paper identity.]

The central EEMD reference is Wu and Huang (2009), which defines ensemble empirical mode decomposition as a noise-assisted method in which finite-amplitude white noise is added over repeated trials and ensemble averaging is used to reduce mode mixing and obtain a scale-organized decomposition. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047] Flandrin, Rilling, and Goncalves (2004) and Wu and Huang (2004) are also authoritative for filtering claims because they connect EMD behavior to filter-bank-like and noise-scale behavior, which is the main basis for describing intrinsic mode functions as adaptive scale bands rather than preassigned physical modes. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref003][@Ref004][DOI:10.1109/LSP.2003.821662][DOI:10.1098/rspa.2003.1221]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Wu and Huang (2009) is the primary EEMD reference, confirmed at DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047; Flandrin et al. (2004) and Wu and Huang (2004) are widely cited for the dyadic filter-bank interpretation of IMFs, confirmed at DOI:10.1109/LSP.2003.821662 and DOI:10.1098/rspa.2003.1221 respectively; all characterizations are consistent with [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

For engineering filtering rather than basic decomposition, Boudraa and Cexus (2007) and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) are more directly relevant because they treat EMD as a signal filtering or denoising tool and address component selection or thresholding in measurement and signal-processing contexts. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref005][@Ref006][DOI:10.1109/TIM.2007.907967][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2009.2013885] Huang and Wu (2008) serves as a useful review source because it summarizes Hilbert-Huang transform applications in geophysical records and discusses open methodological problems, making it appropriate for limitation language in an engineering report. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref007][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Boudraa and Cexus (2007) and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) are peer-reviewed publications addressing EMD-based filtering and IMF-domain denoising, confirmed at DOI:10.1109/TIM.2007.907967 and DOI:10.1109/TSP.2009.2013885; Huang and Wu (2008) is a review article in Reviews of Geophysics confirmed at DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228; all characterizations are consistent with [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

For transient acceleration histories, the EMD and EEMD sources are best paired with strong-motion processing references. Boore and Bommer (2005) documents the consequences of filtering, baseline correction, and filter choices on accelerograms and response spectra, providing the primary framework for evaluating whether any adaptive filtering preserves engineering-relevant signal content. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref008][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007] Boore (2005) addresses pad and filter processing of strong-motion data and is particularly relevant to end-effect and integration behavior. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref009][DOI:10.1785/0120040160] Boore and Akkar (2003) documents how causal and acausal filter choices affect elastic and inelastic response spectra derived from processed accelerograms, demonstrating that spectral consequences of filtering can be significant even at periods well below the filter corner frequency. ### SLOT 2: Supported Component Removal Practices

The literature supports component removal in EMD and EEMD only as a documented filtering or detrending operation followed by reconstruction, not as automatic evidence that the removed or retained intrinsic mode functions are physically unique blast-wave components. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref001][@Ref002][@Ref003] EMD and EEMD decompositions are adaptive and reconstructive, so a processed history can be formed by summing selected intrinsic mode functions and, when appropriate, a residual term; however, the selection criterion must be stated as an engineering or signal-processing rule, such as removal of components dominated by high-frequency noise, removal of a low-frequency trend, or retention of components inside a target response band. A defensible report therefore presents intrinsic mode function removal as scale-selective filtering based on evidence from the record, not as a decomposition into uniquely named source mechanisms. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref007][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The requirement for a stated selection criterion is grounded in the foundational EMD and EEMD sources and the filter-bank interpretation, all cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md]; the restriction against claiming physical uniqueness follows directly from the data-driven adaptive character of the method.]

Removal of early, high-frequency intrinsic mode functions is supported when those components are shown to be dominated by measurement noise or by frequencies outside the engineering response range of interest. Boudraa and Cexus (2007) supports EMD-based signal filtering using partial reconstruction from selected intrinsic mode functions, and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) supports denoising approaches that treat intrinsic mode function content with thresholding logic rather than a blanket rule that the first intrinsic mode function is always noise. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref005][@Ref006][DOI:10.1109/TIM.2007.907967][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2009.2013885] Removal of a residual or trend term can be defensible when the residual represents baseline drift or nonphysical low-frequency contamination, but the same operation can also remove real long-period motion; in acceleration-history applications, such removal must therefore be treated as a high-pass or trend-removal choice whose consequences are checked in integrated velocity, displacement, and response spectra. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref008][@Ref009][@Ref010][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160][DOI:10.1002/eqe.299]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The claim that high-frequency IMF removal requires demonstrated dominance by noise or out-of-band content is supported by Boudraa and Cexus (2007) and Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009) as cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md]; the consequence-checking requirement for residual removal is grounded in the strong-motion processing literature of Boore and Bommer (2005), Boore (2005), and Boore and Akkar (2003).]

Selection of nonadjacent or middle intrinsic mode functions is supported only when the selection is tied to a stated diagnostic, such as frequency content, energy contribution, correlation with the target signal, response-spectral relevance, or comparison with independent instrumentation or physical modeling. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref011][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2007.910389] Rilling and Flandrin (2008) showed that EMD separation of nearby frequency content depends on signal conditions, so component selection cannot be assumed to isolate every true modal contribution in a short transient record. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref011][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2007.910389] EEMD reduces mode mixing by adding noise-assisted ensemble averaging, but the method still depends on ensemble size, added-noise amplitude, sifting choices, and endpoint treatment, so retained intrinsic mode function groups are better described as selected scale bands than as verified physical modes. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][@Ref007][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The requirement for a stated diagnostic before selecting middle IMFs is consistent with the signal-condition dependence shown by Rilling and Flandrin (2008) at DOI:10.1109/TSP.2007.910389; the scale-band description is supported by Wu and Huang (2009) and Huang and Wu (2008); all consistent with [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

### SLOT 3: Known Limitations To Report

Mode mixing is a primary limitation to report for any EEMD-filtered engineering history. EEMD was proposed specifically to reduce the tendency of EMD to place disparate scales in the same intrinsic mode function or split a similar scale across several intrinsic mode functions, but its remedy uses added white noise and ensemble averaging rather than a deterministic physical separation. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047] The reported processing must therefore identify the added-noise level, ensemble size, sifting or stopping criterion, randomization policy where relevant, and any post-processing or alignment method used to compare intrinsic mode functions across trials. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][@Ref007][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Mode mixing and the EEMD noise-assisted remedy are accurately described, consistent with Wu and Huang (2009) at DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047; the parameter disclosure requirements follow directly from the method definition and are supported by Huang and Wu (2008) at DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228; both are cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

End effects, non-uniqueness, and parameter sensitivity are also material limitations that must be disclosed in any report using EEMD-filtered records. EMD envelopes are built from local extrema, so behavior near the beginning and end of a short record can be sensitive to interpolation and boundary extension choices; this is especially important for transient vibration histories in which the main pulse, decay tail, or baseline segment may occur near a boundary. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref001][@Ref007][DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228] The decomposition is not a fixed orthogonal transform with a predeclared basis, and different sifting, stopping, noise, and endpoint choices can change the intrinsic mode function set; therefore the same measured history can yield different but plausible component partitions, which means that reported results are conditional on the stated parameter set. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref003][@Ref002][DOI:10.1109/LSP.2003.821662][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: End-effect sensitivity is accurately characterized; Huang et al. (1998) and Huang and Wu (2008) discuss envelope interpolation and boundary behavior; Flandrin et al. (2004) and Wu and Huang (2009) address the non-fixed, data-driven nature of the decomposition; all consistent with [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

Loss of physical interpretability is a limitation whenever intrinsic mode functions are used for report-level engineering claims. EMD and EEMD components are derived from local time-scale behavior, so the existence of an intrinsic mode function group does not by itself demonstrate a separate blast source, wave path, soil layer response, sensor artifact, or structural mode. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref001][@Ref011][DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2007.910389] Filtering can also alter peak values, apparent pulse duration, low-frequency drift, and response demands; those consequences are well documented in the strong-motion filtering and baseline processing literature and are relevant when processed acceleration histories are used downstream in geotechnical or structural assessment. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref008][@Ref010][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1002/eqe.299]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The absence of inherent physical meaning in EMD/EEMD-derived components is accurately stated and supported by Huang et al. (1998) and Rilling and Flandrin (2008) as cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md]; the downstream consequences on peak values and response spectra are documented in Boore and Bommer (2005) and Boore and Akkar (2003), confirming the relevance of this limitation for engineering applications.]

### SLOT 4: Validation Checks For EEMD-Filtered Histories

Validation of an EEMD-filtered acceleration history is strongest when it compares the raw record, the retained reconstruction, and the removed residual content in both time and frequency domains. Peak preservation checks include the signed and absolute peak acceleration, peak timing, pulse polarity, and local pulse shape near the peak, because a filter that improves visual smoothness can still alter the controlling demand in a transient history. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref008][@Ref009][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160] Baseline and drift checks include numerical integration to velocity and displacement, comparison of terminal velocity and displacement trends, and review of any low-frequency residual removed from the reconstruction. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref008][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The peak preservation and baseline drift checks are consistent with strong-motion processing guidance in Boore and Bommer (2005) and Boore (2005), as cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md]; the time-and-frequency-domain comparison framework is standard practice in signal quality assessment and well grounded in the cited sources.]

Energy and frequency-content checks are defensible because EMD filtering is scale-selective. Comparison of root-mean-square acceleration, summed squared acceleration, cumulative signal energy, Fourier or power spectral density estimates, Hilbert marginal spectra where used, and the frequency content of removed intrinsic mode functions confirms that the operation removed the intended scale range rather than suppressing engineering demand. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref004][DOI:10.1109/TIM.2007.907967][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2009.2013885][DOI:10.1098/rspa.2003.1221] Response-spectrum checks are important when the processed acceleration is used as an engineering input, because response spectra can be sensitive to filtering choices and the difference may be consequential even when the acceleration trace appears acceptable in the time domain. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref010][@Ref008][DOI:10.1002/eqe.299][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Energy and spectral checks are supported by Boudraa and Cexus (2007), Kopsinis and McLaughlin (2009), and Wu and Huang (2004), all cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md] for scale-selective filtering; response-spectrum sensitivity is documented by Boore and Akkar (2003) and Boore and Bommer (2005).]

Repeatability and visual diagnostics are also necessary for EEMD-filtered histories. Because EEMD depends on added-noise amplitude and ensemble averaging, the filtered result can be checked by rerunning the decomposition with the same documented parameters and, where relevant, with alternative random seeds or nearby ensemble settings to confirm that retained scale bands and validation metrics are stable under parameter variation. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][@Ref007][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228] Visual review includes overlaid raw and filtered acceleration traces, removed-component traces, endpoint behavior, pre-event or post-event baseline segments when available, and plots of cumulative residuals or integrated histories, so that the report demonstrates whether the reconstruction preserves the features that control engineering response. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref008][@Ref009][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007][DOI:10.1785/0120040160]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The repeatability check for EEMD is consistent with Wu and Huang (2009) and Huang and Wu (2008), as cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md]; the visual diagnostic recommendations are grounded in the strong-motion processing literature of Boore and Bommer (2005) and Boore (2005); all characterizations are well supported.]

### SLOT 5: Description Without Overclaiming

EEMD filtering is appropriately described in an engineering report as an adaptive, data-driven filtering and reconstruction procedure applied to a measured vibration history. A conservative description states that selected intrinsic mode functions were retained or removed according to documented engineering criteria, and that the resulting reconstruction was accepted only after checks on peak response, energy, frequency content, baseline behavior, and response spectra. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][@Ref005][@Ref008][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047][DOI:10.1109/TIM.2007.907967][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007] The description avoids stating that retained modes are physically unique blast-wave components unless separate evidence from instrumentation, site physics, wave-propagation modeling, or independent measurements supports that interpretation. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref001][@Ref011][DOI:10.1098/rspa.1998.0193][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2007.910389]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of EEMD as adaptive, data-driven filtering is directly supported by Wu and Huang (2009), Boudraa and Cexus (2007), and Boore and Bommer (2005); the requirement for additional evidence before claiming physical uniqueness of retained modes is consistent with Huang et al. (1998) and Rilling and Flandrin (2008), all cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

Appropriate terminology for report use includes "retained intrinsic mode function group," "removed scale band," "EEMD-filtered reconstruction," "adaptive denoising," and "trend or residual removal," because those terms describe the processing operation without asserting a source mechanism. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref003][@Ref006][DOI:10.1109/LSP.2003.821662][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2009.2013885] Terms such as "true blast component," "physical mode," or "isolated wave type" require additional support and are not justified by the decomposition alone, because EMD separation depends on signal conditions and EEMD depends on noise-assisted ensemble choices. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref011][@Ref002][DOI:10.1109/TSP.2007.910389][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The recommended terminology is consistent with how the cited sources describe IMF groups and scale bands; the warning against using "physical mode" or "isolated wave type" without additional evidence is well grounded in Rilling and Flandrin (2008) and Wu and Huang (2009), both cited in [KB:T3.compile.research.md].]

A publication-ready limitation statement can note that EEMD was used to reduce unwanted scale content while preserving defined engineering measures, and that the filtered record is a processed representation of the measured history rather than a recovered unique physical waveform. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref005][@Ref008][DOI:10.1109/TIM.2007.907967][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2004.10.007] The report can further state that conclusions based on the filtered history are conditional on the documented EEMD parameters, component-selection rule, and validation results, and that raw and filtered histories remain distinguishable in the record archive and in subsequent calculations. [KB:T3.compile.research.md][@Ref002][@Ref007][DOI:10.1142/S1793536909000047][DOI:10.1029/2007RG000228]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The limitation statement language is drawn from [KB:T3.compile.research.md] and is grounded in the cited sources; the conditionality statement is consistent with the parameter-sensitivity limitations documented by Wu and Huang (2009) and Huang and Wu (2008); no overclaiming is present.]
