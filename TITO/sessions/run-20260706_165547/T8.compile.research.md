# Guidance Documents for Blasting Effects on Tailings Storage Facilities: AR-S2J2J.R

---

### SLOT 1: Authoritative Guidance Documents Covering Blasting Effects on TSFs

The international industry standards that most directly address dynamic loading, vibration monitoring, pore-pressure response, and liquefaction screening for tailings storage facilities are the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM, 2020) and the IFC Environmental, Health, and Safety Guidelines for Mining (2007). The GISTM, developed under the auspices of the United Nations Environment Programme, the International Council on Mining and Metals, and the Principles for Responsible Investment, requires operators to establish an interdisciplinary knowledge base encompassing geotechnical and seismicity information, to document design criteria explicitly, and to implement integrated performance monitoring throughout the facility life cycle. [@Ref001] The standard does not prescribe fixed numerical vibration thresholds for blast-induced loading on TSFs; it frames safety as a risk-management obligation demanding project-specific assessment and continuous monitoring as the means of demonstrating performance. The IFC EHS Guidelines for Mining (2007) require that tailings impoundments and dams be planned, designed, and operated such that geotechnical risks are assessed and managed throughout the mine life cycle, and they require designs to reflect internationally recognised engineering practices and local seismic conditions, treating geotechnical risk assessment - including blast-induced ground motion effects on tailings facilities - as a distinct discipline rather than an extension of building-vibration compliance. [@Ref001]

The ANCOLD Guidelines on Tailings Dams, Revision 1 (2019), published by the Australian National Committee on Large Dams, provide the most comprehensive single-document treatment of dynamic and seismic loading for tailings storage facilities among the available authoritative sources. [@Ref002] The 2019 revision explicitly aligns tailings dam seismic design criteria with the companion ANCOLD Guidelines for Design of Dams and Appurtenant Structures for Earthquake (2019), addresses both static and cyclic liquefaction of saturated tailings, and recommends conservatively assuming that static liquefaction will occur for brittle, contractive, saturated tailings unless site-specific evidence supports a less conservative classification. The document addresses planning, design, construction, operation, and closure, treating earthquake loading and factor-of-safety requirements under dynamic conditions as subjects requiring engineering analysis throughout the facility life cycle rather than as compliance thresholds satisfied by reference to a look-up table.

ICOLD has produced a series of bulletins that collectively constitute the authoritative international technical foundation for seismic assessment of tailings dams. ICOLD Bulletin 98 (1995), Tailings Dams and Seismicity, addresses the seismic behavior of tailings facilities specifically, covering liquefaction potential, dynamic stability, and modes of failure associated with cyclic ground motion. ICOLD Bulletin 104 (1996), Monitoring of Tailings Dams, establishes the framework for instrumented monitoring programs, including piezometric surveillance of pore-pressure response in TSF embankments. ICOLD Bulletin 121 (2001), Tailings Dams - Risk of Dangerous Occurrences, documents lessons from actual failures including seismically triggered and liquefaction-related events. ICOLD Bulletin 148 (2011), Selecting Seismic Parameters for Large Dams, provides guidance on seismic hazard characterisation and the selection of design earthquake parameters applicable to large storage facilities including tailings dams. [@Ref003] ICOLD Bulletin 194 (2022), Tailings Dam Safety, is the most recent unified ICOLD statement on tailings dam governance and engineering, consolidating seismic requirements alongside operational and monitoring obligations in a form intended to supersede and integrate the earlier bulletins for contemporary professional practice. [@Ref004]

ISO 4866:2010, Mechanical Vibration and Shock - Vibration of Fixed Structures, provides the foundational measurement methodology for blast-vibration monitoring programs. [@Ref001] The standard applies to structures above and below ground including earth structures such as dykes and embankments, and addresses sources including explosions and construction activities; however, it explicitly excludes certain special structures, including dams, from its scope. Because a TSF is typically classified as a dam for regulatory and engineering purposes, ISO 4866:2010 is directly applicable as the technical framework for blast-vibration transducer placement, waveform acquisition, and frequency-content characterisation in TSF monitoring programs, while falling short of providing structural acceptance criteria for the facility itself. The standard's exclusion of dams from its direct scope is relevant to report framing: ISO 4866:2010 supports the measurement methodology but does not establish whether a measured vibration level is acceptable for a particular TSF, which remains a matter of project-specific engineering assessment.

The geotechnical liquefaction-assessment literature constitutes an integral part of the analytical framework for evaluating the response of saturated tailings to blast-induced dynamic loading, even though that literature was developed primarily for earthquake loading scenarios. Youd et al. (2001), summarising the consensus reached at the 1996 NCEER and 1998 NCEER/NSF workshops on evaluation of liquefaction resistance of soils, established the simplified stress-based framework in which the cyclic stress ratio (CSR) representing dynamic demand is compared with the cyclic resistance ratio (CRR) representing soil resistance to produce a factor of safety against liquefaction triggering. [@Ref005] Idriss and Boulanger (2008), Soil Liquefaction During Earthquakes, updated and refined those procedures with revised magnitude scaling factors, improved handling of low-plasticity fine-grained soils of the type commonly encountered in tailings, and updated CPT and SPT-based CRR correlations. [@Ref006] Both frameworks are designed for earthquake-induced cyclic loading, and their application to blast-induced ground motion requires project-specific adaptation of the loading characterisation, particularly the estimation of equivalent uniform cycles associated with a transient blast event.

The Mining Association of Canada's Guide to the Management of Tailings Facilities, Version 3.1 (2019), provides an operations-oriented framework for TSF safety that complements the design-phase guidance of ANCOLD and ICOLD. [@Ref007] The MAC guide structures TSF management through an Operation, Maintenance and Surveillance (OMS) manual framework that requires documented monitoring of all identified performance indicators, including instrumented pore-pressure and vibration responses where blasting occurs in proximity to a TSF. While the MAC guide does not prescribe fixed quantitative vibration thresholds for blast events, its OMS framework establishes the operational mechanism by which project-specific monitoring trigger levels and alarm thresholds are defined, communicated to operators, and updated in response to observed performance.

### SLOT 2: South African versus International Applicability of Sources

Within the South African regulatory environment, the primary legislative instrument governing mining blasting is the Mine Health and Safety Act 29 of 1996 (MHSA), which requires mine operators to prepare and implement a site-specific mandatory Code of Practice addressing health and safety risks associated with their particular operations. The Department of Mineral Resources and Energy issued the Guideline for a Mandatory Code of Practice for Minimum Standards on Ground Vibrations, Noise, Air-blast, and Flyrock near Surface Structures and Communities to be Protected in August 2024, establishing the current national baseline for blast-vibration control in South African mining under sections 9(2) and 9(3) of the MHSA. [@Ref008] This guideline defines protected structures and communities in the context of surface mining, and operators must demonstrate compliance through a site-specific COP that describes monitoring arrangements and acceptable vibration levels for identified sensitive receivers. The 2024 DMRE blasting guideline is South African in origin and legally mandated, making it the primary regulatory compliance reference for blasting vibration limits in a South African mining project, including projects where blasting occurs in proximity to a TSF.

The South African National Committee on Large Dams (SANCOLD) has produced guidance specifically directed at the South African context for tailings dam management. The SANCOLD document "Your Tailings Dam" (2020) addresses design, operation, and safety monitoring of tailings facilities within the South African legislative environment and explicitly notes two significant gaps in the local regulatory framework: first, that South African legislation does not prescribe the severity or return period of a seismic event that a tailings dam must withstand, leaving that determination as a matter of engineering judgement by the designer and owner; and second, that South African legislation does not yet directly address the GISTM requirements. [@Ref009] These observations are directly relevant to report framing: a project operating under South African jurisdiction cannot assume that the seismic or dynamic-loading acceptance criteria in any international standard have been adopted directly into South African law, and the engineer of record must state the basis on which any seismic performance target has been chosen.

The international sources - GISTM, ANCOLD (2019), ICOLD bulletins 98, 104, 121, 148, and 194, the IFC EHS Guidelines for Mining, and ISO 4866:2010 - are applicable across jurisdictions as statements of good international practice and are accepted as technical reference documents for independent review in South Africa. [@Ref001] [@Ref010] Their adoption in a South African TSF report is appropriate as technical framing for engineering assessment, and lenders, financiers, and independent review panels routinely require alignment with GISTM and ICOLD guidance. However, the specific numerical targets in those documents - such as the ANCOLD minimum factor of safety of 1.1 under seismic loading, or earthquake return periods referenced in ICOLD Bulletin 148 - derive from Australian or international regulatory contexts and should be treated as engineering references rather than as South African legal requirements unless the project permit, lender technical schedules, or the Engineer of Record explicitly adopts them as binding project standards.

The U.S. Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement rules in 30 CFR Part 816 (Sections 816.67 and 816.68) are a mature regulatory example of how a blasting framework distinguishes fixed PPV thresholds for standard occupied structures from a project-specific approval process for other structures, including dams, impoundments, utilities, and underground mines. [@Ref001] Under 30 CFR 816.67, the vibration limits for dams and impoundments are not automatically derived from the tabulated building thresholds but are submitted in the blasting plan and approved by the regulator on a project-specific basis. This regulatory architecture - separate fixed limits for standard receivers and a project-specific submission process for engineered earth structures - illustrates the principle that South African practice approximates through the MHSA COP process, even though the South African framework is articulated differently and does not reproduce the specific numerical values or definitional conventions of the U.S. regulations. Citing 30 CFR in a South African TSF report is appropriate as an international regulatory analogue illustrating best-practice structure, provided the report clearly identifies it as a U.S. federal rule rather than as a standard applicable in South Africa.

### SLOT 3: Quantitative Acceptance Criteria versus Project-Specific Assessment

None of the primary authoritative sources for TSF safety - GISTM, ANCOLD (2019), ICOLD bulletins 98, 104, 121, 148, and 194, or the IFC EHS Guidelines for Mining - prescribes a universal fixed blast-vibration threshold expressed as a limiting peak particle velocity for protecting a tailings storage facility. The absence of a universal vibration limit for TSFs reflects the established recognition in geotechnical dam engineering that the dynamic response of a saturated tailings facility depends on material properties, fabric, degree of saturation, drainage conditions, confining stress, pore-pressure history, the geometry of the embankment, and the proximity and characteristics of the blasting source. None of these site-specific factors are captured by a generic PPV criterion derived from research on vibration effects in rigid or masonry building structures. [@Ref001] [@Ref002]

ANCOLD (2019) provides the most explicit quantitative framing available for TSF seismic performance, but its criteria are expressed in terms of structural stability factors of safety and design earthquake return periods rather than in terms of blast-vibration amplitude. The guidelines prescribe a minimum factor of safety of 1.1 for transient and seismic loading conditions, and recommend evaluating the facility against a 1:10,000 annual exceedance probability earthquake where liquefaction of tailings is likely to affect the stability of the embankment, unless a site-specific risk assessment demonstrates that an adequate risk profile is achieved with a lower seismic loading. [@Ref002] These quantitative targets are outputs of a project-specific engineering process - geotechnical characterisation, in-situ testing, liquefaction potential assessment, and dynamic stability analysis - and are not simplified look-up values. Applying them to blast-induced loading requires translating the blast-vibration record into equivalent dynamic demand parameters consistent with the geotechnical analysis framework, which involves project-specific assumptions about wave attenuation, loading duration, and equivalent cyclic cycles.

Liquefaction screening for saturated tailings under dynamic loading is performed within the simplified stress-based framework of Youd et al. (2001) or Idriss and Boulanger (2008), in which the cyclic stress ratio (CSR), representing the demand imposed by the dynamic event, is compared with the cyclic resistance ratio (CRR), representing the soil resistance to liquefaction triggering derived from in-situ penetration tests such as the SPT or CPT. [@Ref005] [@Ref006] The factor of safety against liquefaction is defined as:

$$FS_L = \frac{CRR_{7.5} \cdot MSF \cdot K_\sigma}{CSR}$$

where $CRR_{7.5}$ is the cyclic resistance ratio normalised to a reference earthquake magnitude of 7.5, $MSF$ is the magnitude scaling factor correcting for earthquake duration, and $K_\sigma$ is the overburden correction factor. When blast-induced loading rather than earthquake loading is the triggering mechanism, the CSR must be estimated from the blast-induced ground motion waveform and a representative equivalent number of uniform loading cycles must be assigned to the transient event - a parameter that is far smaller for a blast than for a sustained earthquake of similar peak amplitude. This adaptation of the earthquake-based framework to blast loading requires project-specific assumptions on loading characterisation that are not codified in any universally adopted standard, making $FS_L$ a conditional engineering output dependent on those documented choices.

Pore-pressure monitoring programs serve as the empirical counterpart to analytical liquefaction screening. ICOLD Bulletin 104 (1996) and the MAC OMS framework require piezometer instrumentation within TSF embankments and tailings deposits to measure in-situ pore pressure, detect excess pore-pressure generation following dynamic events, and track pore-pressure dissipation rates. [@Ref007] The monitoring data serve to calibrate and validate analytical models, to detect anomalous pore-pressure responses that may indicate incipient instability, and to confirm that measured vibration events do not produce lasting pore-pressure accumulation above the thresholds defined in the OMS manual. Acceptance criteria for pore-pressure response are project-specific, defined within the OMS manual or instrumentation response plan as trigger values and alarm thresholds derived from site-specific stability calculations rather than adopted from any generic standard or building-vibration criterion.

### SLOT 4: Report Framing to Distinguish Criteria, Monitoring Requirements, and Assumptions

A professional technical report on the effects of nearby blasting on a TSF must clearly delineate four distinct categories of statement: published reference criteria drawn from identified standards; project-specific criteria established for the facility in question by the Engineer of Record; monitoring requirements arising from regulatory obligations, best-practice standards, and design documentation; and analysis assumptions made in the assessment. Conflating these categories - in particular, applying a criterion developed for a different receiver type, such as a building-damage PPV threshold from a vibration standard, as though it governs TSF structural performance - misrepresents the state of professional practice and may produce technically indefensible conclusions that expose the report to challenge during regulatory review or independent assessment. [@Ref001] [@Ref004]

Published reference criteria from building-vibration standards such as BS 7385-2:1993 and from human-comfort and human-exposure standards such as BS 6472-2:2008 or ISO 2631-2 do not govern TSF structural performance and are outside the scope of the present assessment. Those documents are explicitly scoped to building structures and to occupant physiological response respectively; none establishes an acceptance criterion for an earth structure such as a tailings embankment or impoundment. Presenting a PPV threshold derived from those standards as the criterion for protecting a TSF implies a scope of applicability that those standards do not claim, and it obscures the absence of a published fixed vibration threshold for the engineering question actually under assessment. ISO 4866:2010, which does include earth structures within certain parts of its scope, provides measurement and evaluation methodology but explicitly excludes dams and similarly does not prescribe limiting vibration amplitudes for earth embankments or tailings facilities. [@Ref001] The report should therefore name these standards only in their correct application context - as references for the building-damage or human-comfort dimensions of any broader assessment - and should present the TSF structural-performance question as governed by the geotechnical assessment framework described by ANCOLD, ICOLD, GISTM, and the site-specific engineering analysis.

The GISTM (2020) and IFC EHS Guidelines for Mining (2007) frame TSF safety as a risk-management process in which operators define performance criteria embedded in facility design documentation and the OMS manual, rather than a compliance checklist satisfied by reference to tabulated limits. [@Ref001] Within that framework, a project-specific blasting acceptance criterion for the TSF - expressed as a vibration amplitude limit, a pore-pressure trigger level, or a stability factor-of-safety threshold - is established through site-specific engineering assessment, agreed with the independent tailings review board or qualified person, and documented in the OMS manual. The report should name the document in which the project-specific criterion is established, state the basis on which it was derived (for example, the geotechnical analysis from which the pore-pressure trigger value follows, or the stability calculation from which the vibration limit was back-analysed), and distinguish it from both the general guidance documents and from the MHSA COP regulatory compliance thresholds, which address different protected receivers under different legal obligations.

Monitoring requirements constitute a distinct reporting category that should be presented as performance-verification tools operating alongside the analytical assessment, not as substitutes for it. ISO 4866:2010 provides the technical basis for the vibration monitoring methodology, specifying transducer placement, signal acquisition requirements, frequency-content characterisation, and data analysis procedures applicable to blast events affecting earth structures. [@Ref001] ICOLD Bulletin 104 provides the framework for piezometric monitoring programs at tailings dams, and the MAC OMS framework prescribes the structure of an instrumentation response plan including defined trigger and alarm levels. These documents establish what must be measured and how, and they inform the thresholds at which measurements require a response; they do not themselves generate the acceptance criteria, which arise from site-specific engineering calculation.

Analysis assumptions constitute the fourth category requiring transparent reporting. Where the earthquake-based liquefaction-screening frameworks of Youd et al. (2001) or Idriss and Boulanger (2008) are adapted to blast-induced cyclic loading, the project-specific choices that determine the computed factor of safety include the equivalent number of uniform cycles assigned to the blast event, the attenuation relationship used to estimate ground motion at the TSF, the representative penetration resistance profile used to derive CRR, the drainage assumptions applied to the tailings under the transient loading duration, and the specific CRR correlation selected. [@Ref005] [@Ref006] The report should state these assumptions explicitly, distinguish them from established values in the referenced standards, and present the sensitivity of the conclusions to key parameter choices, so that the engineering judgement embedded in the analysis is transparent and auditable. The absence of a codified blast-liquefaction screening procedure in any of the authoritative guidance documents means that the report cannot defer responsibility for those assumptions to a named standard; they are engineering choices that require explicit justification.

### SLOT 5: Source-Backed Terminology for TSF Response to Blasting

The term "dynamic loading" appears in the ICOLD bulletins on tailings dams and in the ANCOLD guidelines with a consistent meaning: any time-varying force or acceleration applied to the structure, its embankment, or its foundation, encompassing earthquake-induced ground motion, blast-induced ground vibration, and in some formulations operational loads from heavy equipment. [@Ref002] [@Ref004] In the tailings dam literature, dynamic loading is treated as a stability-relevant load case that must be considered alongside static loading in the design and performance assessment of the facility and that may govern design where the tailings or foundation material is susceptible to strength loss under cyclic excitation. The term does not carry an implication about the source of the loading; it is applicable to both seismic and blast events provided the dynamic nature of the loading is established.

The term "transient vibration" is used in the vibration measurement literature, including ISO 4866:2010, to describe a vibration event of finite duration that does not persist as continuous or steady-state excitation. [@Ref001] Blast-induced ground motion constitutes a transient vibration event: it consists of a short-duration stress-wave sequence generated by detonation, characterised by a rapidly rising wavefront, a frequency spectrum determined by the charge weight, delay pattern, and geological propagation path, and a decay envelope that is a function of the scaled distance to the receptor. The term is appropriate for characterising the nature of the blast-induced ground motion as a physical input to the TSF assessment but carries no connotations about the structural response or the stability outcome; those are assessed through the geotechnical analysis.

"Pore-pressure generation" and "excess pore pressure" are the standard geotechnical terms for the increase in pore-water pressure above the in-situ hydrostatic equilibrium produced by undrained cyclic or transient loading. ICOLD Bulletin 98 (1995) and the Idriss and Boulanger (2008) monograph use this terminology to describe the fundamental mechanism through which dynamic loading may degrade the effective stress and shear strength of saturated tailings, with the normalised excess pore-pressure ratio defined as:

$$r_u = \frac{\Delta u}{\sigma'_{v0}}$$

where $\Delta u$ is the excess pore-water pressure generated by the dynamic event and $\sigma'_{v0}$ is the initial effective vertical stress. The ratio $r_u$ is the key performance indicator in both instrumented piezometric monitoring and in constitutive models used for dynamic numerical analysis. As $r_u$ approaches unity, the effective stress approaches zero and the undrained shear strength is substantially reduced, with the consequence for stability depending on whether the residual state is contractive or dilative.

"Cyclic mobility" and "flow liquefaction" are the two principal distinct failure modes associated with excess pore-pressure generation in saturated soils under cyclic loading, and their distinction is essential for accurate TSF reporting. Flow liquefaction occurs in contractive soils - including loose, saturated, fine-grained tailings - where the residual undrained shear strength mobilised after triggering is insufficient to maintain stability under the existing static shear stresses, resulting in a catastrophic, sustained loss of resistance and runout. Cyclic mobility occurs in soils that retain a positive residual undrained shear strength after triggering, producing transient loss of effective stress and large cyclic strains without a sustained flow failure. [@Ref011] ICOLD Bulletin 98 and the Idriss and Boulanger (2008) framework distinguish these mechanisms, and ANCOLD (2019) recommends conservatively assuming flow liquefaction for saturated, brittle, contractive tailings absent evidence supporting a less severe classification. [@Ref002] In a technical report, the failure mode being assessed should be named explicitly, and the material evidence - penetration resistance profile, gradation, plasticity index - that supports the classification should be cited.

"Liquefaction potential" is used in the Youd et al. (2001) framework and in the ICOLD tailings dam bulletins to describe the susceptibility of a saturated soil deposit to liquefaction triggering under a specified dynamic loading, with the quantitative measure expressed as the factor of safety against triggering:

$$FS_L = \frac{CRR_{7.5} \cdot MSF \cdot K_\sigma}{CSR}$$

where $CRR_{7.5}$ is the cyclic resistance ratio corrected to a reference earthquake magnitude of $M_w = 7.5$, $MSF$ is the magnitude scaling factor that adjusts for the duration and equivalent cycles of the actual event, and $K_\sigma$ is the overburden correction factor. When a blast event rather than an earthquake provides the dynamic demand, the CSR must be estimated from the blast-induced ground motion parameters and a representative equivalent number of uniform stress cycles must be assigned to the transient loading, making $FS_L$ a conditional quantity whose value is governed by these project-specific input choices. Values of $FS_L > 1$ indicate that the resistance exceeds the demand for the stated assumptions, while $FS_L \leq 1$ indicates a triggering condition; the margin separating these states should be reported alongside the governing assumptions.

"Deformation" and "stability" are used in the ANCOLD guidelines and ICOLD bulletins as distinct but related performance parameters for tailings dam assessment under dynamic loading. Stability refers to the resistance of the TSF embankment and its foundation to slope failure, evaluated as a factor of safety against global instability; ANCOLD (2019) prescribes a minimum value of 1.1 under transient seismic conditions as a quantitative performance target. [@Ref002] Deformation refers to the permanent displacements accumulated during a dynamic event, evaluated through analyses such as the Newmark sliding-block method or fully coupled dynamic numerical analysis; it is possible for a structure to remain nominally stable - maintaining a factor of safety above unity throughout the event - while accumulating permanent deformations that damage the crest, impair drainage elements, or compromise the freeboard required for safe operation. A technical report should distinguish stability assessment from deformation assessment and should state explicitly which analyses have been performed, which have been omitted, and why.

"Monitoring" in the TSF context encompasses instrumented surveillance programs designed to detect developing adverse conditions before they progress to a breach or failure event. [@Ref007] [@Ref001] The GISTM and the IFC EHS Mining Guidelines require integrated performance monitoring programs that include piezometric measurement of pore pressure within the embankment and tailings mass, geotechnical surface monitoring of crest and slope displacement, and vibration monitoring of blast events affecting the facility. In the specific context of blasting effects on a TSF, monitoring serves two distinct functions: a compliance-verification function, demonstrating that measured blast-vibration parameters do not exceed the project-specific thresholds documented in the OMS manual and required by the MHSA COP; and a geotechnical feedback function, providing measured pore-pressure response data that can be compared with model predictions and used to update or validate the analytical assessment of liquefaction potential. A report that presents monitoring results should identify which of these functions the presented data serve and should confirm that the monitoring instrumentation, acquisition parameters, and analysis method are consistent with the requirements of the applicable standard or guidance document, including ISO 4866:2010 for vibration data and ICOLD Bulletin 104 for piezometric data.
