# Blast Record Selection Metrics for Tailings Storage Facility Scenario Analysis: Intensity Measures for Pore-Pressure Accumulation and Liquefaction

The selection of measured blasting records for tailings storage facility (TSF) scenario analysis targeting pore-pressure accumulation and liquefaction potential requires intensity measures and record descriptors that are physically linked to the mechanism of cyclic shear strain accumulation in saturated fine tailings, rather than to the structural damage assessment criteria for which most blast vibration monitoring standards were developed. The conventions established under applicable blast vibration standards and regulatory frameworks for peak particle velocity monitoring and charge-per-delay reporting provide the compliance and monitoring context but do not constitute a complete framework for pore-pressure-oriented record selection. [KB:T1.compile.research.md]

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The framing that PPV-based blast monitoring standards address structural damage and human annoyance rather than pore-pressure generation is well supported by T1.compile.research.md, which documents ISO 4866, BS 7385-2, BS 6472-2, and 30 CFR 816.67 as structural and human-exposure criteria, and explicitly identifies geotechnical effects on tailings impoundments as a separate engineering question requiring independent risk assessment.]

### SLOT 1: Supported Intensity Measures and Record Descriptors

The intensity measure most commonly specified in blast vibration standards and regulatory frameworks is peak particle velocity, but the geotechnical literature establishes that this measure does not characterize pore-pressure accumulation adequately when used in isolation. The selection of blast records for saturated tailings scenario analysis requires measures that capture amplitude, frequency content, duration, and cumulative energy, because each property contributes independently to the cyclic pore-pressure generation mechanism. A review of candidate descriptors in the geotechnical dynamic analysis and blast-vibration literature identifies ten categories with varying degrees of support for evaluating pore-pressure generation and liquefaction potential.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The claim that PPV alone is insufficient for pore-pressure characterization is supported by the systematic intensity measure comparison studies cited in subsequent paragraphs. The four-dimensional characterization of pore-pressure demand (amplitude, frequency, duration, cumulative energy) is consistent with the physical framework of cyclic shear strain accumulation reviewed throughout the geotechnical liquefaction literature.]

Component peak acceleration, defined as the absolute maximum of the time-varying acceleration $a(t)$ over the record duration and denoted $PGA$, is the descriptor most extensively cited in strong-motion earthquake databases and structural design codes. Its applicability to pore-pressure assessment is, however, among the weakest of all candidate descriptors. A systematic study comparing 62 ground motion intensity measures against measured pore water pressure development in saturated sandy soils ranked $PGA$ 61st out of the 62 measures investigated, meaning that almost every other scalar derivable from the same record outperforms $PGA$ as a pore-pressure predictor [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. The physical explanation is that pore-pressure generation under undrained cyclic loading is a cumulative process driven by repeated shear strain cycles above a material-specific threshold, while $PGA$ records only the amplitude of the single largest acceleration ordinate without accounting for the number, duration, or accumulated energy of the cycles that precede or follow that peak.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The ranking of PGA as 61st out of 62 intensity measures for pore-pressure prediction is directly cited from the Tsai et al. study [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. The physical explanation linking PGA weakness to the cumulative nature of pore-pressure generation is consistent with the threshold shear strain framework of Dobry et al. (1982), referenced in the cyclic threshold shear strain literature [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9410%281994%29120%3A12%282208%29].]

Component peak velocity, identified as $PPV$ in blast vibration monitoring and $PGV$ in earthquake engineering, has a stronger physical justification than $PGA$ for pore-pressure-related assessments. The relationship between particle velocity $v$ and the induced cyclic shear strain $\gamma$ in a soil element traversed by a shear wave is given approximately by $\gamma \approx v/V_s$, where $V_s$ is the shear wave velocity of the medium, and this connects the measured surface velocity to the Dobry et al. (1982) threshold shear strain concept for pore-pressure initiation [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9410%281994%29120%3A12%282208%29]. The threshold shear strain for clean saturated sands has been measured at approximately 0.01% across many laboratory investigations, and empirical blast-induced liquefaction field experiments have established threshold $PPV$ values below which residual pore pressure is not generated and above which liquefaction was approached in loose saturated specimens [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0000846]. The Canadian Liquefaction Experiment (CANLEX) and the controlled field tests by Rollins et al. (2005) confirmed that the spatial distribution of measured $PPV$ correlates with the observed extent of pore-pressure generation during controlled explosive detonations [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228670098_Canadian_Liquefaction_Experiment_CANLEX_Blast-Induced_Ground_Motion_and_Pore_Pressure_Experiments][WEB:https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0361198105193600124]. The limitation of $PPV$ as a comprehensive predictor is that once the threshold is exceeded, the magnitude of the excess pore-pressure ratio achieved depends on the number of loading cycles, the cumulative energy, and the drainage conditions, none of which are represented by a single peak velocity ordinate.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The $\gamma \approx v/V_s$ wave mechanics relationship is standard. The 0.01% threshold shear strain for clean sands is cited from the ASCE cyclic threshold shear strains study [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9410%281994%29120%3A12%282208%29]. The CANLEX and Rollins et al. field experiments are directly cited. The limitation about cumulative effects is a well-established conceptual point in the cyclic loading literature.]

Significant duration is supported as a relevant descriptor for pore-pressure assessment because the total number of loading cycles delivered to the soil above the threshold shear strain depends jointly on the amplitude and the duration of shaking. Significant duration is defined conventionally as the elapsed time between the instants at which the cumulative squared acceleration integral reaches specified lower and upper fractions of its total value; the $D_{5-95}$ and $D_{5-75}$ conventions are most commonly cited. Significant duration has been validated as an independent predictor of cumulative damage measures in earthquake ground motion research [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-021-01139-4], and its physical rationale in the context of liquefaction is reinforced by the observation that the simplified stress-based framework of Seed and Idriss (1971) requires both the cyclic stress ratio and the number of applied cycles to determine whether liquefaction is triggered, with the number of cycles closely linked to the effective duration of loading at the relevant amplitude [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. For blast-induced loading, significant duration is typically of the order of tens to hundreds of milliseconds per detonation event, substantially shorter than for earthquake records of comparable amplitude, which limits the cycle budget from a single blast event.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition and use of significant duration as a cumulative damage predictor is well established and cited from a Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering study [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-021-01139-4]. The connection to the Seed-Idriss number-of-cycles framework is cited from Idriss and Boulanger [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. The characterization of blast duration as shorter than earthquake duration is qualitative but consistent with standard blast engineering practice.]

Cycle count is the descriptor most directly tied to the mechanism of pore-pressure accumulation under the cyclic stress or cyclic strain framework. The concept of equivalent uniform cycles, introduced by Seed and Idriss (1971) and refined by Idriss and Boulanger (2008, 2010), reduces an irregular shear stress time history to a reference number of sinusoidal cycles at a standardized amplitude, chosen to produce the same cumulative pore-pressure response or liquefaction probability as the original irregular record [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. A reference amplitude of 0.65 times the peak cyclic shear stress is used, and approximately 15 equivalent uniform cycles are associated conventionally with a $M_w = 7.5$ earthquake. Cycle counting applied to a measured blast acceleration record, using rain-flow counting or zero-crossing counting of the shear stress or shear strain time history, provides a direct estimate of the cycle budget delivered to the saturated tailings, which can then be compared with the cyclic resistance curve of the tailings material.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equivalent uniform cycles concept and the 0.65-amplitude convention are well documented in the Idriss and Boulanger report [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. The Mw = 7.5 / 15 cycles correspondence is a widely cited reference point. Application of rain-flow or zero-crossing counting to blast records is a methodological extension supported in principle by the framework but not validated against a specific blast-and-tailings dataset in the cited sources.]

Dominant frequency is a supported descriptor for record selection because the pore-pressure response of a tailings deposit is sensitive to whether the loading frequency content falls within the range that the deposit transmits and amplifies. Research on seismic record screening for tailings dam time-history analyses found that tailings dams act as low-pass filters and that spectral power accumulated in the 0 to 5 Hz window showed $R^2 = 0.81$ correlation with computed demand, substantially outperforming conventional peak-amplitude measures [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The dominant frequency of a measured blast acceleration time history, identified as the frequency at which the Fourier amplitude spectrum reaches its maximum, provides a compact characterization of whether the blast record is likely to couple efficiently with the natural frequency of the tailings deposit. For typical production blasts at distances relevant to a TSF, dominant frequency commonly ranges from 10 to 100 Hz or above, substantially exceeding the 0 to 5 Hz range relevant to tailings dam response, so an explicit comparison between the dominant frequency of the selected record and the site fundamental frequency is a necessary step in evaluating the physical relevance of the record.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The spectral power in the 0-5 Hz window result and $R^2 = 0.81$ are cited directly from Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The typical dominant frequency range for production blast records (10-100 Hz) is a qualitative characterization consistent with blast-vibration practice but not attributed to a specific study here; this portion is MEDIUM confidence. The overall conclusion about the need for frequency comparison is well supported.]

Bandwidth, as the range of frequencies over which the blast record maintains significant spectral energy, is a subsidiary frequency descriptor relevant to record selection. A narrow-band blast record concentrated above the site fundamental frequency delivers little energy in the frequency range relevant to tailings shear wave amplification even if its peak amplitude is high, while a broader-band record may include components in the low-frequency range that couple more efficiently with the site. The spectral power approach for tailings dam record selection accounts for bandwidth implicitly by integrating the spectral amplitude over the frequency range not filtered by the dam [ARXIV:2009.06426], so bandwidth is subsumed into the spectral power metric rather than treated as an independent criterion. As a standalone descriptor, bandwidth is better used as a diagnostic check on whether the blast record has any meaningful energy content in the pass-band of the tailings deposit.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The spectral power approach that subsumes bandwidth is cited from Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The description of bandwidth as diagnostic rather than primary is a logical inference from the spectral power framework. No specific study validates bandwidth as a standalone pore-pressure predictor for blast records in tailings.]

Arias intensity is among the most extensively supported cumulative energy measures for evaluating pore-pressure generation and liquefaction potential. Proposed by Arias (1970) as an instrumental measure proportional to the integral of the squared ground acceleration over the significant duration, Arias intensity captures amplitude, frequency content, and duration simultaneously because the squaring operation weights high-amplitude cycles more strongly and integration accumulates contributions from all cycles [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arias_intensity]. A liquefaction triggering criterion formulated in terms of Arias intensity, calibrated against $SPT$ and $CPT$ field performance data, established a lower-bound $I_A \approx 0.22$ m/s below which liquefaction is unlikely even in the loosest soils [WEB:https://www.nzsee.org.nz/db/2003/Print/Paper094p.pdf]. Energy-based pore-pressure generation models in which pore-pressure ratio is expressed as a function of cumulative strain energy proportional to $I_A$ have been developed and validated against cyclic laboratory tests and centrifuge models [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JGGEFK.GTENG-11402][WEB:https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6412/15/1/7]. Research addressing tailings liquefaction under dynamic loading found that Arias intensity provides physically reasonable correlations with computed pore-pressure response, though with scatter that motivates the search for higher-fidelity alternatives that also incorporate frequency content [ARXIV:2204.11393].

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Arias intensity is a well-established measure with multiple authoritative citations spanning its original definition [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arias_intensity], its liquefaction threshold application [WEB:https://www.nzsee.org.nz/db/2003/Print/Paper094p.pdf], energy-based pore-pressure model validation [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JGGEFK.GTENG-11402][WEB:https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6412/15/1/7], and tailings-specific application [ARXIV:2204.11393]. Confidence is HIGH across all claims in this paragraph.]

Cumulative absolute velocity ($CAV$), defined as the integral of the absolute value of acceleration over the total record duration, has documented empirical support as a predictor of pore-water pressure development. A systematic comparison of 62 intensity measures against laboratory pore-pressure measurements in saturated sandy soils found that cutoff-$CAV$ formulations, in which acceleration intervals below a threshold of approximately 0.02 to 0.04g are excluded from the integration, were among the strongest predictors of pore-pressure ratio, significantly outperforming both the original $CAV$ without cutoff and $PGA$ [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. Probabilistic liquefaction triggering and manifestation models calibrated against the $CAV$ and validated against field case histories confirmed that $CAV$-based criteria provide a reliable alternative to the conventional cyclic stress ratio approach, particularly for ground motions difficult to characterize by a single spectral ordinate [WEB:https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0002729]. For blast acceleration records, $CAV$ can be computed directly from the time history after baseline correction and provides a cumulative loading measure that captures both the amplitude and duration of the blast pulse.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The 62-measure comparison study is cited from Tsai et al. [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. Probabilistic liquefaction models using CAV are cited from an ASCE JGGE study [WEB:https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0002729]. The standard CAV definition and its computation from baseline-corrected records are well established.]

Root-mean-square acceleration $a_{RMS}$ is a secondary cumulative descriptor that normalizes the energy content of the record by the significant duration. It is physically related to Arias intensity and significant duration through the identity $a_{RMS} = \sqrt{2g I_A / (\pi D_{5-95})}$, making it sensitive to both the amplitude of loading and its temporal concentration. For pore-pressure applications in saturated tailings, $a_{RMS}$ is supported as a secondary descriptor that complements cycle count or Arias intensity by providing a normalized measure of the average loading intensity over the active shaking window; it does not independently account for the frequency content of the loading, and its use as a primary selection criterion is not established in the blast-tailings literature.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The mathematical relationship between aRMS, Arias intensity, and D5-95 is a standard algebraic identity derivable from definitions. The characterization of aRMS as secondary and lacking frequency-content representation is a logical inference. The claim that aRMS is used in blast-vibration characterization is consistent with practice but not cited from a specific study here.]

Response spectra carry limited direct support for predicting pore-pressure accumulation at the material level but are relevant for characterizing structural demand on the dam. The 5%-damped pseudo-spectral acceleration $S_a(T)$ at a given period describes the peak response of an equivalent single-degree-of-freedom oscillator, which is physically different from the cyclic shear stress or shear strain experienced by a soil element under undrained loading. For blast records applied to TSF scenario analysis, response spectra are most relevant as a diagnostic tool for matching the dynamic demand of the selected record to the fundamental period of the tailings deposit. The spectral power approach of Labanda et al. (2021), which integrates spectral amplitude over the frequency band passed by the dam, extends this reasoning into a selection criterion substantially more robust than any single spectral ordinate [ARXIV:2009.06426]. Response spectra computed from processed blast records should also be verified after any EEMD-based or other filtering of the time history, because filtering choices can alter spectral ordinates in ways that are consequential even when the peak acceleration trace appears acceptable [@Ref010][@Ref008] [KB:T3.compile.research.md].

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between structural spectral demand and material-level cyclic shear stress is a well-established point in geotechnical earthquake engineering. The spectral power approach is cited from Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The sensitivity of response spectra to filtering is documented in T3.compile.research.md [KB:T3.compile.research.md] with the [@Ref010][@Ref008] citekeys preserved from that source.]

Fourier amplitude spectra provide a frequency-domain characterization of the blast record that is essential for evaluating whether the record's energy content is relevant to the dynamic response of the tailings deposit. The Fourier amplitude spectrum reveals the distribution of energy across frequencies, from which the dominant frequency, bandwidth, and relative weight of energy in each frequency band can be assessed. In the context of processing blast records by EEMD-based filtering, comparison of the Fourier or power spectral density estimates of the raw and filtered records provides a necessary validation check to confirm that the filtering operation removed the intended scale content and did not suppress components in the frequency range relevant to tailings response [@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref004] [KB:T3.compile.research.md]. Fourier spectra are a diagnostic and descriptive tool; they do not by themselves quantify cumulative loading demand and should be used in combination with energy-based measures such as Arias intensity or $CAV$ rather than as standalone selection criteria.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The use of Fourier spectra for frequency-content characterization is standard practice. The validation role of Fourier spectra in EEMD-filtered record checking is supported by T3.compile.research.md [KB:T3.compile.research.md] with the [@Ref005][@Ref006][@Ref004] citekeys preserved. The characterization of Fourier spectra as diagnostic rather than standalone is consistent with the spectral power literature [ARXIV:2009.06426].]

### SLOT 2: Metric Formulas and Governing Assumptions

Component peak acceleration and component peak velocity are defined directly from the measured time histories. Peak acceleration is:

$$PGA = \max_{t \in [0,\, T]} |a(t)|$$

where $a(t)$ is the component acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$ (or in units of $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$) and $T$ is the total record duration. Peak velocity is obtained by integrating the baseline-corrected acceleration:

$$PGV = \max_{t \in [0,\, T]} |v(t)|, \quad v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(\tau)\, d\tau$$

where $v_0$ is the initial velocity, set to zero for records collected at rest. The assumptions embedded in these definitions are that the record has been corrected for baseline offset (the mean of the pre-event window is zero), that the correct component is selected and its convention is stated (geometric mean, maximum of three orthogonal components, or vector resultant), that the sensor has not been clipped at its full-scale amplitude, which can occur in near-field blast records, and that the sampling rate is sufficient to capture the true peak of the velocity pulse. Any mean offset in the acceleration record accumulates during integration and can produce a spurious velocity trend, so baseline correction before integration is a non-negotiable pre-processing requirement. [KB:T3.compile.research.md]

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The PGA and PGV/PPV definitions are universally accepted. The baseline correction requirement for velocity integration is documented in the strong-motion processing literature, including sources cited in T3.compile.research.md [KB:T3.compile.research.md] at [@Ref008][@Ref009]. The component convention issue is well known in blast monitoring practice as referenced in T1.compile.research.md [KB:T1.compile.research.md].]

Significant duration $D_{5-95}$ is derived from the cumulative Arias energy integral. Define the running Arias energy as:

$$E(t) = \int_0^t a^2(\tau)\, d\tau$$

with total energy $E_T = E(T)$. The lower boundary time $t_5$ satisfies $E(t_5) = 0.05\, E_T$ and the upper boundary time $t_{95}$ satisfies $E(t_{95}) = 0.95\, E_T$, so:

$$D_{5-95} = t_{95} - t_5$$

The $D_{5-75}$ convention applies the same procedure with boundaries at 5% and 75% of $E_T$, yielding a shorter duration estimate that excludes the low-energy tail of the record. The governing assumptions are that the record is long enough to capture the full energy accumulation so that $E(T) = E_T$ rather than a partial integral, that the pre-event baseline is flat and does not contribute falsely to the cumulative energy, and that the chosen percentage convention is documented explicitly. For short blast records, the choice between $D_{5-95}$ and $D_{5-75}$ can change the reported significant duration substantially, and consistency with the duration convention used in any referenced correlation or calibration must be maintained.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The D5-95 definition from the cumulative squared acceleration integral is standard in earthquake engineering. The assumptions about pre-event baseline and record completeness are standard requirements. The note about D5-75 as a shorter convention is correct and widely used.]

The number of equivalent uniform cycles $N_{eq}$ at a reference cyclic stress ratio is obtained from the irregular shear stress time history $\tau(t)$ by a cycle-counting procedure that identifies $n$ cycles at amplitudes $\tau_i$, then applies a damage-weighted summation:

$$N_{eq} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} N_i \left(\frac{\tau_i}{\tau_{ref}}\right)^b$$

where $N_i$ is the count of cycles at amplitude $\tau_i$, $\tau_{ref} = 0.65\,\tau_{max}$ is the Seed and Idriss (1971) reference amplitude, and $b$ is a material-dependent exponent related to the slope of the cyclic resistance curve in log-log space, typically in the range 2 to 3 for sandy soils [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. The governing assumptions are that the cyclic resistance follows a power-law relationship between cyclic stress ratio and cycles to liquefaction, that the cycle-counting algorithm correctly captures the shear stress amplitude distribution, and that $b$ is known from laboratory testing of the specific tailings material. For blast records, the shear stress $\tau(t)$ at the depth of interest must be computed from the surface acceleration record using a site response or wave propagation model because direct measurement of subsurface shear stress is generally not feasible.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The equivalent uniform cycles formula is cited from Idriss and Boulanger [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. The exponent $b$ range of 2 to 3 is consistent with published calibrations for sands but has not been verified for fine tailings specifically. The requirement for site response analysis to obtain subsurface shear stress is an important practical assumption. MEDIUM confidence because the tailings-specific exponent is not cited directly.]

Dominant frequency $f_d$ is defined as the frequency at which the Fourier amplitude spectrum $|A(f)|$ reaches its maximum:

$$f_d = \arg\max_{f \geq 0}\, |A(f)|$$

where:

$$|A(f)| = \left|\int_0^T a(t)\, e^{-2\pi i f t}\, dt\right|$$

is the one-sided Fourier amplitude spectrum of the acceleration record. An alternative definition is the zero-crossing frequency, which counts the number of zero-crossings per unit time in the accelerogram and divides by two, giving a mean cycle frequency that is less sensitive to spectral leakage than the discrete Fourier peak. The centroid frequency $f_c = \int f\, |A(f)|\, df / \int |A(f)|\, df$ provides a weighted average that is less sensitive to a single spectral peak and more representative of the overall energy distribution. All three definitions require that the record has been baseline-corrected before the transform is applied, because a non-zero mean offset introduces a spurious zero-frequency component that distorts the apparent spectral shape.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Fourier amplitude spectrum definition and the dominant, zero-crossing, and centroid frequency conventions are standard signal-processing procedures consistent with the processing conventions discussed in T3.compile.research.md [KB:T3.compile.research.md]. No specific external study is required for these well-established definitions.]

Arias intensity $I_A$ is defined as:

$$I_A = \frac{\pi}{2g} \int_0^T a^2(t)\, dt$$

where $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, and the result has units of m/s [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arias_intensity]. The integration runs over the full record duration $T$ from pre-event baseline through post-event decay. The governing assumptions are that the record is free of significant baseline error, that $T$ is long enough that truncation at the end does not substantially reduce $E_T$, and that the component convention is consistent with any calibrated triggering criterion being applied: $I_A$ is component-specific and criteria calibrated against the geometric mean of horizontal components should not be applied to a single-component or resultant measurement without adjustment. For blast records, the typically short significant duration means that $I_A$ from a single detonation event is likely to be much lower than from an earthquake record of comparable peak amplitude.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Arias intensity formula and units are standard and universally accepted [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arias_intensity]. The assumptions regarding baseline correction, record completeness, and component convention are well-established requirements for any integration-based intensity measure. The implication for short blast records is a direct consequence of the formula.]

Cumulative absolute velocity is defined as:

$$CAV = \int_0^T |a(t)|\, dt$$

with units of m/s [WEB:https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0002729]. The standardized variant $CAV_5$, developed originally for nuclear facility applications, modifies this definition by summing the integral of $|a(t)|$ only over those one-second time intervals within the record in which the peak absolute acceleration exceeds $0.025g$; intervals with $a_{max} < 0.025g$ are excluded from the summation. The governing assumptions for basic $CAV$ are that the record has been baseline-corrected and that the component convention is stated. For $CAV_5$, the additional assumption is that the one-second interval convention is appropriate for the record type. Because the $CAV_5$ convention was calibrated against earthquake records with durations of tens of seconds, application to blast records whose total significant duration is a fraction of one second requires care: the entire significant energy of the blast may be contained within a single one-second window, in which case $CAV_5$ reduces to the basic $CAV$ computed over that window and the acceleration threshold convention serves no meaningful filtering function. The result should be checked against the basic $CAV$ and both values reported when they differ.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The CAV definition and CAV5 convention are cited from the ASCE liquefaction study [WEB:https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0002729]. The argument that CAV5 reduces to basic CAV for sub-second blast records is a logical inference from the one-second interval definition applied to short events; this specific claim has not been verified against a published blast record processing example, so MEDIUM confidence is appropriate.]

Root-mean-square acceleration is defined over the significant duration interval $[t_5,\, t_{95}]$ as:

$$a_{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{D_{5-95}} \int_{t_5}^{t_{95}} a^2(t)\, dt}$$

with units of $\text{m/s}^2$. An equivalent expression in terms of Arias intensity and significant duration is:

$$a_{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{2g\, I_A}{\pi\, D_{5-95}}}$$

which holds when $I_A$ is computed over the same $[t_5,\, t_{95}]$ interval. When $I_A$ is computed over the full record and $D_{5-95}$ is the standard significant duration, this identity is an approximation because the D5-95 interval excludes the 10% energy tails of the record. The assumption embedded in this formula is that the integration limits are consistent: $I_A$ and $D_{5-95}$ must be computed over the same time window for the identity to hold exactly.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The aRMS definition and its algebraic relationship to Arias intensity and D5-95 are mathematically correct standard identities. The note about integration limit consistency is a well-known technical detail in strong-motion processing.]

The 5%-damped pseudo-spectral acceleration $S_a(T, \zeta = 0.05)$ at natural period $T$ is the maximum absolute acceleration response of a linear single-degree-of-freedom oscillator with natural circular frequency $\omega_0 = 2\pi / T$ and fraction of critical damping $\zeta = 0.05$ subjected to the base acceleration $a(t)$. The governing equation of motion is:

$$\ddot{u}(t) + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot{u}(t) + \omega_0^2 u(t) = -a(t)$$

and the spectral ordinate is $S_a(T) = \max_t |\ddot{u}(t) + a(t)|$, in units of $\text{m/s}^2$. The assumptions are that the oscillator is linear elastic, that damping is rate-independent viscous, and that 5% damping is appropriate for the structure or material being characterized. For saturated tailings under blast-induced cyclic loading, which involves material nonlinearity, pore-pressure-induced stiffness reduction, and rapid frequency content, the linear elastic assumption is a significant simplification; response spectra should therefore be treated as approximate demand indicators for record selection purposes rather than as direct predictors of material-level pore-pressure response.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The SDOF equation of motion and spectral acceleration definition are textbook-standard. The 5% damping convention is universally adopted. The limitation of the linear elastic SDOF assumption for nonlinear saturated tailings is a well-known conceptual point in geotechnical earthquake engineering.]

The Fourier amplitude spectrum of a digitized acceleration record sampled at time step $\Delta t$ with $N$ total samples is computed using the discrete Fourier transform:

$$A_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} a_n\, e^{-2\pi i k n / N}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$$

where $a_n = a(n\,\Delta t)$, and the one-sided amplitude spectrum is $|A(f_k)| = |\Delta t\, A_k|$ for $f_k = k/(N\,\Delta t)$ with $0 \leq k \leq N/2$. The units of $|A(f)|$ are m (acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$ times duration in s). The governing assumptions are that the record satisfies the Nyquist criterion ($f_{max} = 1/(2\Delta t)$ must exceed the highest frequency of interest), that the time history is free of DC offset before transformation, that any discontinuities at the endpoints are controlled by tapering if spectral leakage is a concern, and that the sampling rate is high enough to avoid aliasing. For blast records containing energy above 100 Hz in the near field, a sampling rate of at least 2000 samples per second is a standard requirement, consistent with Nyquist's theorem applied to the target upper frequency limit.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The DFT formula and its assumptions are textbook-standard signal-processing requirements. The 2000 samples/second guideline for blast records is consistent with standard blast monitoring practice and with the sampling requirements implied by ISO 4866 as discussed in T1.compile.research.md [KB:T1.compile.research.md], though no specific numerical sampling rate recommendation is cited directly; the Nyquist argument is mathematically definitive.]

### SLOT 3: Frequency Content and Duration Representation for Saturated Fine Tailings

The physical mechanism governing pore-pressure accumulation in saturated fine tailings under undrained cyclic loading is the progressive rearrangement of particles when the applied cyclic shear strain exceeds the material threshold shear strain, leading under undrained conditions to an increase in pore water pressure. Fine tailings, by virtue of their low hydraulic conductivity relative to clean sands, drain more slowly once pore pressure is generated, which means that residual excess pore pressure persists for a longer period after the loading event. This drainage behavior is critical for scenario analysis because incomplete pore-pressure dissipation between successive blast events allows cumulative buildup across the operational loading sequence, even if a single event generates only a modest pore-pressure increment [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2018/1921057].

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The threshold shear strain mechanism for pore-pressure generation is foundational in geotechnical earthquake engineering [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9410%281994%29120%3A12%282208%29]. The slower drainage of fine tailings relative to clean sands is a well-established geotechnical property. The four-stage pore-pressure accumulation in tailings under dynamic loading is cited from Jin et al. (2018) [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2018/1921057]. The cumulative multi-event argument is a logical consequence of slow drainage.]

The sensitivity of a tailings deposit to loading frequency arises from the coupled effects of site amplification, wave propagation, and the frequency-dependent relationship between surface-measured vibration and subsurface shear stress. Tailings deposits have relatively low shear wave velocities, commonly in the range of 50 to 200 m/s for loose to medium-dense material, implying fundamental natural periods on the order of 0.5 to 2 seconds (frequencies of 0.5 to 2 Hz) for deposits of typical thickness. Loading energy concentrated at frequencies substantially above the fundamental frequency of the deposit is attenuated through the deposit and does not produce efficient shear wave amplification at depth, while loading energy in the 0 to 5 Hz range passes through and can produce resonant amplification of shear stress in the tailings. Research on seismic record screening for tailings dam analyses confirmed that spectral power accumulated in the 0 to 5 Hz band yielded $R^2 = 0.81$ with computed demand, while conventional peak measures showed substantially weaker predictive capacity [ARXIV:2009.06426].

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The shear wave velocity range for loose to medium-dense tailings (50-200 m/s) and implied fundamental frequency range are physically consistent with published geotechnical characterization data for tailings deposits. The spectral power result is directly cited from Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. Low-pass filter behavior of the dam is documented in that study.]

For blast records specifically, the dominant frequency of the ground motion typically exceeds the fundamental frequency of the tailings deposit by one to two orders of magnitude. Production blast records measured at distances representative of proximity to a TSF commonly exhibit dominant frequencies in the 10 to 100 Hz range depending on charge mass, geometry, and scaled distance, whereas the relevant tailings deposit frequency range is below 5 Hz [ARXIV:2009.06426]. This frequency mismatch means that a blast record selected on the basis of its $PPV$ or $PGA$ may not couple efficiently with the dynamic response of the tailings deposit, and the actual pore-pressure demand delivered to the tailings may be much smaller than implied by the peak amplitude alone. The selection of blast records for tailings scenario analysis should include an explicit check on whether the record has meaningful energy content in the 0 to 5 Hz range, or alternatively, whether a site-specific transfer function should be applied to translate the surface blast ground motion into a representative subsurface shear stress history for the tailings.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The 0-5 Hz range relevance is directly supported by Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The typical 10-100 Hz range for production blast records is consistent with blast engineering practice but is not cited from a specific study here; MEDIUM confidence for the numerical frequency characterization of blast records. The implication of frequency mismatch is a logical consequence of the two frequency ranges.]

The number of loading cycles above threshold shear strain is a function of both the duration and the dominant frequency of the record. For a record with dominant frequency $f_d$ and significant duration $D_{5-95}$, the approximate number of loading cycles in the significant shaking window is $N_{app} \approx f_d \cdot D_{5-95}$. For a typical blast record with $f_d = 20$ Hz and $D_{5-95} = 0.1$ s, this estimate gives approximately 2 loading cycles in the significant window, far fewer than the 15 equivalent uniform cycles associated conventionally with an $M_w = 7.5$ earthquake loading in the simplified liquefaction triggering framework [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. A single blast event is therefore unlikely to accumulate sufficient cycles to trigger liquefaction by the number-of-cycles criterion alone unless the cyclic stress ratio is very high, but repeated blast events with incomplete pore-pressure dissipation between events can deliver cumulative loading equivalent to many more cycles over the operational period.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The approximate cycle count formula $N_{app} \approx f_d \cdot D_{5-95}$ is a first-order estimate and is not attributed to a specific citation. The 15 equivalent uniform cycles for Mw 7.5 is cited from Idriss and Boulanger [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. The numerical example is for illustration; MEDIUM confidence because the representative $f_d$ and $D_{5-95}$ values for blasts near a TSF are illustrative, not cited.]

For fine tailings in particular, experimental evidence from undrained cyclic triaxial tests demonstrates that pore-pressure generation depends on density conditions, the applied cyclic stress ratio, and the number of cycles, with behavior deviating substantially from clean sand models when fines content is elevated [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40703-023-00185-y]. Fine-grained cyclically loaded materials, including silts and tailings, tend to exhibit cyclic mobility behavior in which excess pore pressure does not reach the initial vertical effective stress but still causes significant softening and deformation, meaning that the criterion for structural concern in fine tailings is not necessarily full liquefaction but a serviceability-level pore-pressure ratio that reduces shear strength below the required value for slope stability. Selection criteria for blast records in fine tailings scenario analysis should therefore reflect the target pore-pressure ratio associated with the serviceability or ultimate limit state of the TSF, not only the pore-pressure ratio corresponding to full liquefaction initiation.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The cyclic loading behavior of fine-grained materials including tailings is cited from [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40703-023-00185-y]. The concept of cyclic mobility as distinct from full liquefaction is a well-established distinction in geotechnical practice. The recommendation to target serviceability pore-pressure ratios is a logical extension not directly cited from a specific blast-tailings study; MEDIUM confidence for this forward-looking recommendation.]

Three aspects of frequency content and duration should be addressed when selecting blast records for saturated fine tailings scenario analysis. The dominant frequency or mean frequency of the record should be assessed relative to the fundamental frequency of the tailings deposit, with preference for records whose spectral energy extends into the 0 to 5 Hz range not filtered by the dam [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The $D_{5-95}$ significant duration should be reported as an indicator of the cycle count budget, and the $D_{5-75}$ duration should also be computed to quantify sensitivity to the convention, because the upper tail of a typical blast accelerogram consists of low-amplitude coda that extends $D_{5-95}$ without contributing meaningfully to shear stress in the tailings. The cumulative loading scenario should be characterized by specifying the number of blast events, the inter-event interval, and whether pore-pressure dissipation is expected between events, because the scenario-relevant intensity measure is the cumulative $I_A$ or cumulative $CAV$ delivered over the operational sequence rather than the single-event measure.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The three-aspect framework is logically derived from the physical arguments in the preceding paragraphs, supported by [ARXIV:2009.06426] for frequency content, [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf] for cycle count, and [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2018/1921057] for cumulative multi-event loading. The D5-75 recommendation for blast records is a logical inference from blast record characteristics.]

### SLOT 4: Combined Peak and Cumulative Descriptor Selection

No single scalar intensity measure simultaneously captures all of the physical dimensions relevant to pore-pressure accumulation in saturated fine tailings under blast-induced loading. The instantaneous amplitude dimension, measured by $PGA$ or $PPV$, determines whether the threshold shear strain is exceeded and whether any pore-pressure is initiated, but provides no information about how many times the threshold is exceeded or by what cumulative energy margin. The cumulative energy dimension, measured by Arias intensity or $CAV$, provides information about the total loading budget but does not indicate whether the loading was concentrated in a frequency range that couples with the tailings deposit. The frequency content dimension, characterized by dominant frequency, bandwidth, or spectral power in the relevant band, determines the efficiency of energy transfer from the blast to the tailings, but spectral information without amplitude context does not predict whether the delivered energy exceeds the material resistance. The combination of descriptors from at least two of these dimensions is therefore physically motivated and has a reasonable basis in the geotechnical literature for both earthquake and blast loading contexts.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The tripartite characterization of pore-pressure demand (amplitude, cumulative energy, frequency content) is a logical synthesis of the physical framework reviewed in Slots 1 through 3, supported by the threshold shear strain literature [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9410%281994%29120%3A12%282208%29], the Arias intensity and CAV liquefaction studies, and the spectral power approach of Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The inference that no single scalar captures all three dimensions is well supported by the ranked intensity measure comparison study [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372].]

The spectral power intensity measure proposed by Labanda et al. (2021) for tailings dam record selection is an integrated descriptor that combines amplitude and frequency content within a single quantity. By integrating the squared pseudo-spectral acceleration over the frequency range not filtered by the dam, this measure accounts for the loading energy delivered at the frequencies relevant to the tailings response, which inherently weights both the amplitude of the record in the relevant frequency band and the breadth of frequency content within that band. The resulting correlation with computed demand in finite-element analyses of a representative upstream tailings dam substantially exceeded correlations obtained with $PGA$, $PGV$, Arias intensity, or single-period $S_a$ [ARXIV:2009.06426]. This approach represents the strongest currently available framework for combining spectral and amplitude information in a single scalar selection criterion for tailings dam analyses, although its calibration against earthquake records requires verification of the assumed frequency pass-band when applied to blast records, because blast records have different spectral character than the earthquake records used in the calibration.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The spectral power approach and its comparative performance are directly cited from Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The caution about calibration against earthquake records is a logical methodological limitation consistent with the basis of the study and not contradicted by any cited source.]

A simpler bi-variate approach that is defensible in the absence of a site-specific calibrated spectral power metric is the combination of $PPV$ as the amplitude descriptor with Arias intensity as the cumulative energy descriptor. $PPV$ provides the threshold check: if $PPV$ is below the empirical threshold for pore-pressure initiation in the tailings material, the event does not generate significant pore pressure regardless of $I_A$. If $PPV$ exceeds the threshold, $I_A$ provides an estimate of the cumulative loading delivered by the event, which can be compared with the energy demand required to reach a target pore-pressure ratio using an energy-based pore-pressure generation model [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JGGEFK.GTENG-11402][WEB:https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6412/15/1/7]. This combination mirrors the conditional logic applied in established earthquake engineering selection frameworks: a threshold amplitude criterion first screens records that would produce no relevant response, and an energy-based criterion then screens for cumulative demand matching among the records that pass the amplitude threshold.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The PPV threshold concept is supported by the CANLEX and Rollins et al. experiments [WEB:https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0361198105193600124][WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228670098_Canadian_Liquefaction_Experiment_CANLEX_Blast-Induced_Ground_Motion_and_Pore_Pressure_Experiments]. The energy-based pore-pressure models supporting IA as cumulative descriptor are cited [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JGGEFK.GTENG-11402][WEB:https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6412/15/1/7]. The specific bi-variate PPV + IA framework for blast record selection in tailings is not described in a directly cited published standard; MEDIUM confidence because it is an inference from two independently supported components.]

Cutoff-$CAV$ combined with a frequency content descriptor provides an alternative bi-variate selection strategy. Cutoff-$CAV$, using an acceleration threshold of approximately 0.02 to 0.04g, excludes low-amplitude intervals below the pore-pressure threshold and is among the strongest predictors of pore-pressure ratio identified in the systematic 62-measure comparison [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. Pairing cutoff-$CAV$ with the spectral power in the 0 to 5 Hz range provides a bi-variate criterion that captures both how much cumulative loading is delivered and how efficiently it couples with the tailings deposit frequency range [ARXIV:2009.06426]. This combination has not been formally validated against blast-induced tailings pore-pressure measurements in a published study, so its application requires either site-specific calibration against measured pore-pressure data or explicit documentation as a conservative engineering judgment.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The cutoff-CAV measure is cited from the Tsai et al. study [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. The pairing with spectral power in 0-5 Hz is a logical extension of Labanda et al. [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The absence of a published validation of this specific bi-variate combination for blast-induced tailings pore-pressure is acknowledged, justifying MEDIUM confidence.]

The evidentiary basis for combined descriptor selection specifically in blast-induced loading on fine tailings is limited by the scarcity of published field experiments in which both the full suite of blast record descriptors and the pore-pressure response of fine tailings material have been measured and correlated. The CANLEX project experiments provide direct measurements of $PPV$ and pore-pressure response in loose saturated sand, and the Rollins et al. (2005) controlled field tests provide similar data for loose sandy soils, but neither dataset includes fine tailings specifically nor provides a dataset large enough to calibrate a bi-variate or multi-variate selection criterion [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228670098_Canadian_Liquefaction_Experiment_CANLEX_Blast-Induced_Ground_Motion_and_Pore_Pressure_Experiments][WEB:https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0361198105193600124]. The strongest inference available from the existing literature is that combining a peak amplitude measure for threshold exceedance with a cumulative energy measure for total loading is more physically defensible than either descriptor alone, and this combination should be the minimum standard for record selection in the absence of site-specific calibration data.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The CANLEX and Rollins et al. experiments are directly cited. The characterization of the evidence base as not including fine-tailings-specific blast data is accurate based on the literature reviewed. The recommendation for combined peak plus cumulative criteria as the minimum standard is supported by the physical argument and the cited intensity measure comparison literature.]

### SLOT 5: Weakly Supported or Misleading Descriptors and Stated Limitations

Peak ground acceleration is the descriptor most vulnerable to misuse in blast-induced TSF pore-pressure assessment. Its principal limitation has been established empirically: in a systematic analysis of 62 candidate ground motion intensity measures ranked by their ability to predict pore water pressure development in saturated sandy soils, $PGA$ was ranked 61st, meaning that practically every other measure derivable from the same record outperforms $PGA$ as a pore-pressure predictor [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. The physical explanation is that pore-pressure generation is a cumulative process driven by repeated shear strain cycles, and a single peak amplitude ordinate does not capture whether the loading was sustained for two cycles or two hundred. $PGA$ is acceptable as a coarse threshold screening tool to confirm that the acceleration level exceeds a minimum floor below which no shear strain damage is possible, and as a regulatory compliance reporting metric under blast vibration standards that specify peak acceleration or peak particle velocity limits. It is inappropriate as a primary or sole record selection criterion for representing pore-pressure demand on saturated tailings. The limitation that must accompany any $PGA$-based threshold criterion is that it screens only for the presence or absence of potential pore-pressure initiation and does not characterize the magnitude of pore-pressure accumulation.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The empirical ranking of PGA as 61st out of 62 intensity measures for pore-pressure prediction is directly cited from Tsai et al. [WEB:https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4010372]. The physical explanation and the stated limitation are consistent with the cumulative pore-pressure generation mechanism reviewed throughout this document. The acceptable versus inappropriate distinction is logically derived from the threshold-only role of PGA.]

Peak particle displacement ($PPD$) appears in some blast monitoring reports and seismograph output files but has no documented support for predicting pore-pressure accumulation in tailings or analogous saturated materials. $PPD$ is typically computed by integrating the velocity time history once and characterizes the maximum quasi-static displacement of the soil mass during the blast pulse. For structural damage assessment, peak displacement is relevant in contexts where cracks in masonry or plaster respond to relative displacements, but for the shear-strain-driven mechanism of pore-pressure generation in saturated fine tailings, $PPD$ does not correspond to any physically relevant quantity that determines whether the threshold shear strain is exceeded or how many times it is exceeded. A large $PPD$ can result from a low-frequency, low-amplitude blast whose shear strain at depth is negligible, while a high-frequency, high-PPV event with small $PPD$ can exceed the threshold shear strain many times. $PPD$ may be reported alongside $PPV$ and $PGA$ for completeness of the blast monitoring record, but it should not be used as a selection or scaling criterion for tailings pore-pressure scenario analysis.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The claim that PPD has no documented support for pore-pressure prediction in tailings is supported by the absence of any PPD-pore-pressure correlation in the literature reviewed. The physical explanation (PPD does not correspond to cyclic shear strain in the relevant sense) is a logical inference consistent with wave mechanics. This paragraph relies primarily on the absence of supporting evidence rather than positive evidence of the limitation; MEDIUM confidence is appropriate.]

Response spectral accelerations at periods shorter than the fundamental period of the tailings deposit may be misleading for pore-pressure-oriented record selection. A blast record exhibiting high $S_a$ at periods of 0.1 s or shorter, corresponding to frequencies of 10 Hz or higher, contains energy predominantly in the frequency range that the tailings deposit filters before it reaches the tailings material at depth. Selecting such a record on the basis of spectral amplitude at the wrong period would produce a record rich in energy content that is largely irrelevant to tailings shear stress amplification, while potentially undersampling records with modest short-period spectral ordinates but significant low-frequency content that actually couples with the deposit. The appropriate period range for spectral characterization in tailings scenario analysis, when response spectra are used at all, is the fundamental period of the tailings deposit or the spectral power over the 0 to 5 Hz band [ARXIV:2009.06426]. The stated limitation that should accompany any use of short-period $S_a$ for blast record selection is that spectral ordinates at periods below the site fundamental period characterize energy that the deposit does not amplify and that therefore has limited relevance to the subsurface shear stress demand on the tailings.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The argument that short-period spectral acceleration is misleading for tailings pore-pressure selection is a direct logical consequence of the Labanda et al. findings [ARXIV:2009.06426], which showed that frequency content above the dam's filter cutoff does not contribute to demand. The recommended period range is directly cited from [ARXIV:2009.06426].]

The standardized $CAV_5$ convention, which integrates $|a(t)|$ only over one-second intervals where the peak acceleration exceeds $0.025g$, was calibrated against earthquake records with typical durations of 10 to 100 seconds and was developed originally for nuclear facility safety system applications. When applied to blast records whose significant duration is a fraction of one second, the convention can give misleading results because the entire significant energy of the blast is typically contained within a single one-second window, reducing $CAV_5$ effectively to the basic $CAV$ computed over that one window, so that the filtering function of the acceleration threshold that was intended to exclude weak inter-event intervals in an earthquake record serves no useful purpose. Additionally, if the post-event baseline acceleration falls below $0.025g$ immediately after the blast pulse, $CAV_5$ captures only the blast pulse itself, which is functionally identical to basic $CAV$ truncated to the significant shaking duration. The limitation that must be stated when using $CAV_5$ for blast records is that the one-second interval convention was not calibrated for records of sub-second significant duration and that the standard form should be verified against the basic $CAV$ before reporting.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The CAV5 convention and its intended earthquake application are cited from [WEB:https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0002729]. The argument about CAV5 reducing to basic CAV for sub-second blast records is a logical inference from the definition; this specific limitation has not been verified against a published blast record processing example, so MEDIUM confidence is appropriate.]

The equivalent uniform cycles concept, as calibrated in the simplified liquefaction triggering procedure through earthquake magnitude scaling factors, is not directly transferable to blast records without recalibration. Magnitude scaling factors are derived from statistical relationships between earthquake magnitude and the expected number of equivalent uniform cycles in ground motions, based on the waveform characteristics of earthquake records [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. Blast records have fundamentally different waveform characteristics: they are much shorter in duration, have higher dominant frequencies, and their amplitude decays more rapidly with distance than the coda of a tectonic earthquake. Applying the magnitude scaling factor calibrated for earthquake records to a blast record, and using the resulting $N_{eq}$ in the standard liquefaction triggering chart, may substantially misrepresent the actual cycle equivalent because the controlling difference between blast and earthquake waveforms - short duration versus high frequency - could lead to underestimation or overestimation depending on which dimension of the mismatch dominates in the particular application. The stated limitation is that $N_{eq}$ derived from a blast record using the Seed-Idriss magnitude scaling framework should be treated as an approximation requiring site- and material-specific validation against laboratory cyclic tests on the tailings material rather than as a direct input to a standard triggering chart.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The magnitude scaling factor calibration against earthquake statistics is well documented in Idriss and Boulanger [WEB:https://faculty.engineering.ucdavis.edu/boulanger/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2014/09/Idriss_Boulanger_SPT_Liquefaction_CGM-10-02.pdf]. The qualitative differences between blast and earthquake waveforms are well established in the blast vibration literature. The limitation about potential over- or underestimation of cycle equivalence is a logical inference from these differences not contradicted by any cited source.]

Fourier amplitude spectra, when used as a standalone selection criterion without integration or normalization, are potentially misleading because the raw spectral amplitude at a given frequency does not quantify how much cumulative energy has been delivered at that frequency, nor whether the record as a whole delivers sufficient energy to generate pore-pressure response. A blast record could exhibit a high Fourier amplitude at a particular frequency while delivering little total energy if the pulse is very short, or it could have a broad low-amplitude spectrum while still delivering substantial cumulative energy across many frequency bands. The appropriate use of Fourier spectra in blast record selection for tailings is as a diagnostic tool: to verify that the selected record has energy in the relevant frequency band, to confirm that EEMD or other filtering has not suppressed energy in that band [@Ref005][@Ref006] [KB:T3.compile.research.md], and to compare the frequency content of the candidate record with the site amplification function. Fourier spectra should be presented alongside energy-based measures such as Arias intensity or $CAV$, not as replacements for them.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The limitation of raw Fourier amplitude spectra as standalone selection criteria is a well-established signal-processing point. The diagnostic role of Fourier spectra in validating EEMD-filtered records is cited from T3.compile.research.md [KB:T3.compile.research.md] with preserved [@Ref005][@Ref006] citekeys. The recommendation to use Fourier spectra alongside energy-based measures is consistent with the spectral power framework [ARXIV:2009.06426].]

Scaled distance, which is widely used in blast vibration attenuation relationships to predict $PPV$ at a given distance from a charge of specified mass, is a descriptor of source-receiver geometry rather than of the measured ground motion and should not be used as an intensity measure for record selection purposes. Scaled distance predicts the statistical central tendency of $PPV$ at a given distance and charge mass, but it does not predict the frequency content, duration, waveform shape, or energy content of the resulting motion, all of which vary substantially within the scatter of typical attenuation relationships [KB:T1.compile.research.md]. Two blast records from the same nominal scaled distance may differ significantly in dominant frequency, significant duration, and Arias intensity because of differences in rock properties, blast geometry, initiation sequence, and wave propagation path. For tailings pore-pressure scenario analysis, record selection based on scaled distance matching ensures amplitude consistency only in an average statistical sense and does not guarantee frequency or energy consistency; the stated limitation is that scaled-distance matching is necessary but not sufficient for producing a representative blast record set for pore-pressure-oriented scenario analysis.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between scaled distance as a source-receiver geometry descriptor versus a ground motion intensity measure is fundamental in blast vibration engineering. The limitation of scaled distance for frequency, duration, and energy prediction is well established and consistent with the blast attenuation frameworks discussed in T1.compile.research.md [KB:T1.compile.research.md] in the context of component attenuation models and monitoring requirements.]
